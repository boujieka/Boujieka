# Sacred number and sacred geometry in ancient Greece: Pythagoreanism, Plato, Neoplatonism, isopsephy

Scope: archaic Greece to late antiquity (c. 570 BCE to the 5th century CE). In each section, **[V]** marks a fact verified in the sources fetched, **[T]** a traditional or ancient interpretation (what ancient authors claimed), and **[U]** an unverifiable or legendary claim.

## What do the primary texts say, and what do scholars consider later legend?

### Takeaway
The earliest evidence (Xenophanes, Heraclitus, Herodotus, Ion, Empedocles) shows Pythagoras (c. 570 to c. 490 BCE) as an expert on the soul's fate and transmigration, a ritual authority and the founder of a way of life. It does not show him as a mathematician. Most of the number doctrine (the harmonic ratios, *harmonia*, "all things are known through number") is first found in Philolaus (5th c. BCE) and in Aristotle's reports on the "so-called Pythagoreans". The tetractys, the theorem, the hammer story and the "music of the spheres" credited to Pythagoras himself are later attributions, the method and conclusion of Burkert (1972). Plato's *Timaeus* gives the first systematic sacred geometry (the world-soul built on musical ratios and the elements as regular solids). Neopythagoreans (Nicomachus) and Neoplatonists (Iamblichus, Proclus) later developed arithmology, in which numbers are linked to gods.

### Cited Findings

**Pythagoras: early attestations [V]**
- Pythagoras c. 570 to c. 490 BCE. He was born on Samos, settled in Croton c. 530 BCE, went to Metapontum after violence c. 510 BCE and died there c. 490. He "wrote nothing", and no source from his lifetime or the next 200 years shows any written work by him. — [SEP, "Pythagoras" (Huffman)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)
- Xenophanes (Fr. 7): Pythagoras tells a man to stop beating a puppy because he recognizes a friend's soul in its yelp, which is evidence of metempsychosis. Heraclitus calls him "chief of the charlatans" (Fr. 81) and calls his learning "polymathy, an evil conspiracy" (Fr. 129). Ion of Chios praises his wisdom about the soul's afterlife. Empedocles (Fr. 129) calls him "a man who knew remarkable things". Herodotus (II.81, IV.95) discusses Pythagorean burial custom and Zalmoxis. — [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)
- Porphyry's *Life* ch. 19, which derives from Dicaearchus (4th c. BCE, called the most reliable early source), lists four doctrines: the soul is immortal, souls transmigrate, events recur, and all living beings are kin. — [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)

**Burkert's critique (Lore and Science in Ancient Pythagoreanism, Eng. 1972) [V]**
- Burkert's method treats the earliest evidence as the standard against which later accretions are judged. His conclusion is that Pythagoras was famous as a soul expert, ritual authority, wonder-worker and founder of a way of life, and that mathematics and cosmology were not the basis of his fame. Plato, Aristotle and Dicaearchus never credit him with mathematics. — [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)
- Later attributions, according to the SEP:
  - **The Pythagorean theorem.** No early source mentions it. The first link to Pythagoras comes in two verses attributed to Apollodorus, relayed by Proclus (5th c. CE). There is no evidence of a proof by Pythagoras, and the Babylonians knew the rule earlier.
  - **The harmonic ratios.** Xenocrates (Fr. 9) is the first source that links them to Pythagoras. The blacksmith's-hammer story "is false". The discovery is better credited to Hippasus (experiments with bronze disks) and Philolaus (Fr. 6a).
  - **The music of the spheres and orbital spheres.** There is no early evidence. The first cosmic model of this kind is Philolaus's.
  - **The tetractys.** It is first explicit in Aetius (1st c. CE), though an akousma reported by Iamblichus (VP 82) may go back to Aristotle.
  - **The Monad and Indefinite Dyad.** These appear in the forged *Pythagorean Memoirs* (c. 200 BCE). Aristotle says the indefinite dyad belongs to Plato.
  - **The spherical earth, the five zones and the identity of the morning and evening star.** The earliest evidence credits Parmenides, Oenopides and others.
  - **Geometry.** Eudemus's history of geometry gives Pythagoras no role.
  - [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)
- *Akousmata* ("things heard") are taboo-like maxims about ritual, diet and conduct, and Aristotle is the earliest source that quotes them. The later split into *acusmatici* and *mathematici* is traced to Aristotle by Burkert, but Zhmud argues it is a later construction first found in Clement. The *acusmatici* said Hippasus, not Pythagoras, was the source of the mathematical philosophy. — [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)
- Some ancient testimonies contradict each other: Aristoxenus says Pythagoras ate meat, while Eudoxus says he avoided it entirely. — [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)

**Late biographies: reliability [V]**
- Diogenes Laertius (c. 200 to 250 CE) is the "most objective" of the three late biographers, but his main account relies on the forged *Memoirs*. Porphyry (234 to c. 305) and Iamblichus (c. 245 to 325) have explicitly non-historical agendas. Iamblichus presents Pythagoras as a soul sent by the gods, and Porphyry may set him up as a rival to Jesus. Nicomachus (c. 50 to 150 CE) gives Pythagoras Platonic and Aristotelian metaphysics. — [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)
- Iamblichus's *On the Pythagorean Life* largely borrows from earlier biographies. It presents Pythagoras as a "divine man" who received a revelation from Orpheus through "Aglaophamus". — [SEP, "Iamblichus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/iamblichus/)

**Philolaus and the 5th-century Pythagorean number doctrine [V]**
- Philolaus of Croton (c. 470 to c. 390 BCE) was the first Pythagorean said to have written a book. In his fragments, things are made of limiters and unlimiteds held together by *harmonia* (Frs. 1, 2, 6). The role of number is epistemic: "all things are known through number" (Fr. 4). Odd and even are prominent (Frs. 4 and 5). The ratios 2:1 (octave), 3:2 (fifth) and 4:3 (fourth) are central to his cosmology (Fr. 6a). His cosmos has a central fire and a counter-earth. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- Aristotle (*Metaphysics*) reports that "the Pythagoreans say that things are numbers or that they are made out of numbers". In this report odd is limited and even is unlimited. Aristotle criticizes them for adding a counter-earth so that there would be ten heavenly bodies, because ten is the perfect number, when only nine were observed. Scholars debate whether "things are numbers" is Aristotle's own inference. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- The table of ten opposites reported by Aristotle: limit/unlimited, odd/even, one/plurality, right/left, male/female, rest/motion, straight/crooked, light/darkness, good/bad, square/oblong. It has a normative slant (good against bad). — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- Eurytus arranged pebbles to show the number of a man or a horse. Archytas was the first great mathematician of the tradition and was probably the target of Plato's one reference to Pythagoreans, on musical numbers. The story that Hippasus drowned for revealing the dodecahedron is "probably not historical". The last Pythagoreans (at Phlius) died out c. 350 BCE (Aristoxenus). — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)

**The tetractys [V/T]**
- The tetractys is a triangle of ten points in rows of 1, 2, 3 and 4. Read in sequence, the rows give the ratios 4:3, 3:2 and 2:1, the basic intervals of Pythagorean scales. The Pythagorean oath swears by it ("By that pure, holy, four lettered name on high", in a modern rendering that may not be literal). The article does not identify the oath's ancient source. — [Wikipedia, "Tetractys"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetractys)
- Hippolytus (d. c. 235 CE) describes the tetractys as point, line, surface and solid, which sum to the decad. Speusippus (4th c. BCE) wrote *On Pythagorean Numbers*, possibly the first work of arithmology. Zhmud considers the perfection of ten "a Platonic rather than a Pythagorean doctrine". — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)

**Perfect numbers [V]**
- Euclid (*Elements* IX.36) proved that 2^(p−1)(2^p − 1) is an even perfect number whenever 2^p − 1 is prime. Nicomachus (c. 100 CE) knew 6, 28, 496 and 8128. He claimed without proof that every perfect number has this form, and he wrongly said that perfect numbers end alternately in 6 and 8. The terms "perfect", "deficient" and "abundant" come from Greek numerology. Philo (*On the Creation*) said the world was made in 6 days and the moon orbits in 28 days because 6 and 28 are perfect. Augustine (*City of God*) repeated the claim about 6. — [Wikipedia, "Perfect number"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_number)

**Plato: Timaeus and the nuptial number [V]**
- In the *Timaeus*, the world-soul is made of Sameness, Difference and Being, divided in a "harmonically proportionate series". It is split into two crossing circles, the circle of the Same and the circle of the Different, which explain cognition of being and of becoming. Time is the "image of eternity" and is marked by the heavenly bodies. — [SEP, "Plato's Timaeus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-timaeus/)
- The world-soul is built on the diatonic scale prominent in Philolaus (Fr. 6a). This is a specific Pythagorean influence on Plato. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- The elements are regular solids (53b–56c): fire is a tetrahedron, air an octahedron, water an icosahedron and earth a cube. Fire, air and water are built from half-equilateral triangles and can turn into one another. Earth is built from isosceles right triangles and cannot. The dodecahedron is "used for the universe as a whole" (55c). — [SEP, "Plato's Timaeus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-timaeus/)
- The nuptial or "geometrical" number (*Republic* VIII, 546b) is "notoriously difficult", with no real agreement on its meaning or value. Proposed values include 216 (the most frequent; 216 = 3³ + 4³ + 5³ in James Adam's reading, *The Nuptial Number of Plato*, 1891), 3,600 and 12,960,000 (= 3,600² = 4,800 × 2,700). One abstract reports fairly wide agreement on 12,960,000 but dispute over how it is derived. Michael Allen's *Nuptial Arithmetic* (1994) studies Ficino's commentary on the passage. — [Wikipedia, "Plato's number"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato%27s_number); [PhilPapers abstract](https://api.philpapers.org/rec/EHRTWO-2); [UC Press, Allen](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/nuptial-arithmetic-marsilio-ficino-s-commentary-on-the-fatal-number-in-book-viii-of-plato-s-republic.html)

**Neopythagorean arithmology [V]**
- Neopythagoreanism was "a tendency, not a school". It flourished from the 1st c. BCE and merged into Neoplatonism by Iamblichus's time. Its main figures:
  - Eudorus (late 1st c. BCE): a supreme One, a monad and an indefinite dyad.
  - Moderatus (1st c. CE): three "ones".
  - Numenius (c. 150 CE): "What else is Plato than Moses speaking Greek?" (Fr. 8).
  - [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- Nicomachus of Gerasa (first half of the 2nd c. CE) wrote four works relevant here:
  - *Introduction to Arithmetic*, which survives and was paraphrased by Boethius.
  - *Handbook of Harmonics*, which survives. It gives the ratios 2:1, 3:2 and 4:3 and tells the blacksmith-hammer story.
  - *Introduction to Music*, which is lost but is paraphrased in Boethius's *De institutione musica* I–III.
  - *Theology of Arithmetic*, which is known through Photius's summary. It equates the pagan gods with the numbers of the decad, an idea Iamblichus and Proclus took up.
  - [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- *Theologumena Arithmeticae* is an anonymous work on number symbolism that is "sometimes attributed to Iamblichus, but it is not by him". It quotes Nicomachus and Anatolius, Iamblichus's teacher. — [SEP, "Iamblichus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/iamblichus/). The SEP "Pythagoreanism" entry adds that it probably reflects the arithmological book of Iamblichus's *On Pythagoreanism* and relies heavily on Nicomachus. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- Iamblichus's *On Pythagoreanism* ran to ten books. Book III treats mathematics as an aid to grasping the intelligible. Book IV is a commentary on Nicomachus. Books V–VII treat arithmetic in physics, ethics and theology. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- Proclus (410/412 to 485): Marinus (*Life of Proclus* 28) reports that Proclus dreamed he was the reincarnation of Nicomachus. O'Meara describes Proclus's Platonism as still heavily Pythagorean. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- Reception: Calcidius (4th c.) attributed the harmony of the spheres to Pythagoras, and Macrobius (5th c.) attributed to him the idea that the soul is a harmony. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)

**Isopsephy (Greek letter-number values) [V]**
- The Greek alphabetic numerals need 27 signs, so three archaic letters were added to the 24: digamma or stigma (6), qoppa (90) and sampi (900). The system is additive (σμα = 241). Examples of isopsephy:
  - Apollonius of Perga (3rd c. BCE), an early example that uses multiplication.
  - A Pompeii graffito (before 79 CE): "I love her whose number is 545".
  - Suetonius reports a Roman slogan in which Νέρων = 1005 = "he killed his own mother".
  - Leonidas of Alexandria (1st c. CE) wrote isopsephic epigrams.
  - [Wikipedia, "Isopsephy"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isopsephy)
- **Abrasax = 365**: Α1 + Β2 + Ρ100 + Α1 + Σ200 + Α1 + Ξ60 = 365. Irenaeus reports 365 Basilidian heavens whose ruler is Abraxas, who "contains within himself 365 numbers". Hippolytus (*Refutation* VII.26) reports that the Basilidians called their great archon Abrasax because his name totals 365, the days of the year. The magical papyrus PGM VIII links the seven letters to the seven planets and the value 365 to the year. On engraved gems, Abraxas is combined "chiefly, perhaps exclusively" with Mithras among Persian deities. — [Wikipedia, "Abraxas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraxas); [Britannica, "abraxas"](https://britannica.com/topic/abraxas-sequence-of-Greek-letters)
- Many of the figures on "Basilidian" gems show no direct acquaintance with the doctrines of Basilides himself. — [Wikipedia, "Abraxas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraxas)
- **Mithras = 365**: Jerome (*Commentary on Amos*, 3rd book, on Amos 5:9–10, according to Roger Pearse's citation of Geden's translation) suspected that Abraxas was the Persian Mithras, the Sun, and noted that the name Mithras gives the same total with different letters. I relied on secondary citations and did not check the Latin text. — [Roger Pearse, "Mithras and Jerome"](https://roger-pearse.com/weblog/2012/06/02/mithras-and-jerome). The 1911 Schaff-Herzog encyclopedia notes that the 365 value does not explain the name any more than it explains "Meithras" and "Neilos", which have the same value. — [CCEL, Schaff-Herzog](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/encyc01.html?term=abrasax)
- **666 (Revelation 13:18)**: "Let him that hath understanding count the number of the beast: for it is the number of a man." Most manuscripts read 666 (χξϛ). Papyrus 115 (Oxyrhynchus, published c. 2005), the oldest witness to Revelation 13 cited, and Codex Ephraemi read 616. Irenaeus (2nd c.) knew of 616 but kept 666. He proposed Greek isopsephic solutions: ΕΥΑΝΘΑΣ, ΤΕΙΤΑΝ and ΛΑΤΕΙΝΟΣ, which he found "very probable". In Hebrew, נרון קסר (Nrwn Qsr, "Nero Caesar") = 50 + 200 + 6 + 50 + 100 + 60 + 200 = 666. The Latin-form spelling נרו קסר = 616, which explains the variant. A Wadi Murabba'at Aramaic document uses this spelling of Nero's name and title. There is a "broad consensus in contemporary scholarship" that the number refers to Nero. — [Wikipedia, "Number of the beast"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_of_the_beast)

### Inferences
- With the spelling ΜΕΙΘΡΑΣ, Μ40 + Ε5 + Ι10 + Θ9 + Ρ100 + Α1 + Σ200 = 365 (my arithmetic). The usual spelling Μίθρας, without epsilon, gives 360. So the Mithras = 365 equation depends on the spelling Meithras, the one attested by Schaff-Herzog and Jerome.
- The 666/616 pair is better explained by Hebrew gematria (Nero) than by Greek isopsephy. The text of Revelation is Greek, but the variant 616 makes most sense if the original count was made in Hebrew letters. Irenaeus's Greek solutions show that early Christian readers spontaneously applied Greek isopsephy.
- "Pythagorean number mysticism" as popularly described (the tetractys, gods as numbers, a perfect decad) is largely a reconstruction built from Philolaus, Plato and the early Academy (Speusippus, Xenocrates) and from Neopythagoreans of the 1st to 4th centuries CE, then projected back onto Pythagoras.

### Gaps
- The exact numbers of the world-soul in the *Timaeus* (35b–36b): the series 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 8, 27, the harmonic and arithmetic means, the 9:8 tone and the 256:243 *leimma*. These are standard in the primary text but did not appear in the SEP pages I fetched. Check them in a translation (Cornford, Zeyl) or on [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0180) before citing.
- The music of the spheres in Aristotle (*De Caelo* II.9, rejecting the sound of the heavens) and in Plato's Myth of Er (*Republic* X, sirens on the spindle): not verified this session.
- The ancient source of the tetractys oath (often given as Sextus Empiricus, Iamblichus or Aetius): not confirmed.
- Huffman's and Riedweg's monographs were not consulted directly, only through the SEP.

## What spiritual utility did these traditions claim for number contemplation?

### Takeaway
Across the tradition, number and geometry were claimed to purify the soul and re-attune it to cosmic order. Pythagoras taught transmigration and a regulated way of life. Philolaus held that things are known through number. In the *Timaeus*, studying the heavens' revolutions realigns the soul's disturbed circles. For Iamblichus, mathematics practiced "in a Pythagorean manner" is "elevating, purifying and perfective" and prepares the soul for theurgy. For Proclus, learning mathematics is recollection, the soul's ascent from sense to intellect.

### Cited Findings
- Pythagoras prescribed a highly structured way of life (the *akousmata*) and taught metempsychosis. Purification is implied by the ritual taboos, but the SEP excerpt does not discuss it explicitly. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/); [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/)
- *Timaeus*: the soul's revolutions are thrown off course at birth (43a–44b). The goal of life is to realign them with those of the universe (90d) by studying its revolutions and harmonies. This restores the soul's original state, "that most excellent life offered to humankind by the gods, both now and forevermore" (90d). — [SEP, "Plato's Timaeus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-timaeus/)
- Iamblichus (c. 242 to c. 325) treated mathematics as an intermediate realm between the intelligible and the sensible. It is a spiritual discipline (*askēsis*), not merely technical. Practiced "in a Pythagorean manner", it is "divinely inspired, elevating, purifying and perfective". It helps the soul detach from the material world and is a starting point toward higher being. — [SEP, "Iamblichus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/iamblichus/)
- Theurgy (*theourgia*, "godlike action") is co-action with the gods through prayer, offerings and sacrifice. Iamblichus rejected Plotinus's doctrine of an undescended soul. He held that the soul is "broken in its essence" yet "able to regain some ground through its activity", that the gods hold the key, and that the practitioner must be virtuous. He grouped divine entities by meaningful numbers, especially triads (remaining, procession, return). *De Mysteriis* defends ritual against Porphyry's objections. — [SEP, "Iamblichus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/iamblichus/)
- Iamblichus's *On General Mathematical Science* strongly influenced Proclus's *Commentary on the First Book of Euclid's Elements*. — [SEP, "Iamblichus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/iamblichus/)
- Proclus: learning is "nothing else than the soul's recollection of her inherent reasons". Mathematics lifts the soul from sense toward intellect. Imagination (*phantasia*) supplies "intelligible matter" in which the unextended geometrical forms are projected for study. — [T. Taylor's 1792 translation of Proclus, transcription by D. Wilkins (TCD)](https://www.maths.tcd.ie/~dwilkins/Euclid/Proclus_Taylor1792/ProclusCommentaryOnEuclid_TTaylor_ProclusBookIChap01_DraftTranscription.pdf); [MacIsaac, *Dionysius*](https://ojs.library.dal.ca/dionysius/article/view/12719/11388); [Nikulin (Harvard history of math course)](https://legacy-www.math.harvard.edu/archive/hist_206r_2009/nikulin.pdf)
- Nicomachus's *Theology of Arithmetic* equated the gods with the numbers of the decad, which made number contemplation a form of theology. Iamblichus and Proclus developed this further. — [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- In Gnostic and magical contexts, the number 365 coded cosmic totality (the days of the year, 365 heavens), and the seven letters of Abrasax were linked to the seven planets in PGM VIII. — [Wikipedia, "Abraxas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraxas)

### Inferences
- The tradition has a clear arc. It begins with an ethical and ritual way of life (archaic Pythagoreanism). Plato turns it into an epistemic and contemplative assimilation to cosmic order. The Neoplatonists then make it a graded ascent in which mathematics is a purifying intermediate and theurgy is the final step, for Iamblichus. In each phase the claimed benefit is the soul's restoration and its kinship with the divine, not practical prediction.
- Isopsephy was used differently. It served for identification and encoding (names, riddles, magical names, apocalyptic cryptograms) rather than as a contemplative discipline. In the Gnostic and magical case it gave a divine name cosmic power.

### Gaps
- Explicit uses of the term *katharsis* for mathematics in Proclus's Greek text, and O'Meara's *Pythagoras Revived* (1989) and *Platonopolis* (2003), were not consulted directly.
- I did not verify whether Porphyry's *Life of Pythagoras* or Iamblichus's *On the Pythagorean Life* explicitly describe mathematics as a purification (tradition often says so).

## Which popular modern claims (golden ratio everywhere, etc.) are unsupported?

### Takeaway
The ancient Greeks knew the "extreme and mean ratio" as a geometric construction (Euclid, c. 300 BCE). The names "divine proportion" (Pacioli, 1509) and "golden section" (attested from 1789) are early modern, and the symbol φ dates from c. 1910. Claims that the Parthenon, the Great Pyramid or Greek architecture in general was designed on the golden ratio are not supported by measurements, according to Markowsky (1992), Herz-Fischler, Livio (2002), Devlin (2005) and Foutakis (2014). Many claims about Pythagoras himself (the theorem, the hammers, the music of the spheres, the tetractys) are also later legends.

### Cited Findings
- Euclid's *Elements* (c. 300 BCE) contains the first known definition: "A straight line is said to have been cut in extreme and mean ratio…". The earliest known use of "golden section" is J. S. T. Gehler (*Physikalisches Wörterbuch*, 1789), and James Sully used the English term in 1875. Luca Pacioli called it the "divine proportion" (*Divina proportione*, 1509). Mark Barr used φ by about 1910. — [Wikipedia, "Golden ratio"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio)
- Gazalé (1999): "It was not until Euclid … that the golden ratio's mathematical properties were studied." — [Wikipedia, "Golden ratio"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio)
- **The Parthenon**:
  - Livio (2002, pp. 74–75) says the claims "ha[ve] largely been discredited".
  - Devlin (2005, *The Math Instinct*, p. 108): "the oft repeated assertion that the Parthenon in Athens is based on the golden ratio is not supported by actual measurements."
  - Foutakis (2014) measured Greek monuments from the 5th c. BCE to the 2nd c. CE and found the golden ratio "totally absent from Greek architecture of the classical fifth century BC".
  - [Wikipedia, "Golden ratio"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio)
- George Markowsky, "Misconceptions about the Golden Ratio", *The College Mathematics Journal* 23(1), Jan. 1992, pp. 2–19. On the Parthenon he notes that parts of the building (for example the steps of the base) fall outside the golden rectangles that enthusiasts sketch, and that published dimensions vary between sources because they measure from different reference points. Golden-ratio advocacy sites (goldennumber.net) dispute his treatment, so read those critiques with caution. — [Search summary of Markowsky's paper and of the replies on goldennumber.net](https://www.goldennumber.net/golden-ratio-misconceptions-by-george-markowsky-reviewed/?amp). I could not read the full PDF (it was a scanned image).
- **The Great Pyramid**: Herz-Fischler (2000) reports that the consensus of modern scholars is that its proportions are not based on the golden ratio. Markowsky: "It does not appear that the Egyptians even knew of the existence of" the ratio. Rossi (2004): "there is no direct evidence in any ancient Egyptian written mathematical source". Because of measurement imprecision, the golden-ratio theory cannot be distinguished from π-based or whole-number theories. — [Wikipedia, "Golden ratio"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio)
- **Aesthetic preference**: Fechner (1876) reported a preference for golden rectangles, but later careful tests were "at best, inconclusive" (Livio 2002, p. 7). — [Wikipedia, "Golden ratio"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_ratio)
- **Other unsupported popular claims about Pythagoras**: that he proved the theorem, discovered the musical ratios with blacksmiths' hammers (the story "is false"), and taught the music of the spheres. Hippasus's drowning for revealing the dodecahedron is "probably not historical". — [SEP, "Pythagoras"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoras/); [SEP, "Pythagoreanism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pythagoreanism/)
- **The nuptial number** has no agreed solution, so any confident modern "decoding" of it (216, 12,960,000 or others) is an interpretation, not a fact. — [Wikipedia, "Plato's number"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato%27s_number)

### Inferences
- The "golden ratio everywhere" narrative depends on three things: Renaissance and 19th-century names projected back onto antiquity; flexible choice of measurement points on buildings; and the conflation of Euclid's purely geometric treatment (the pentagon, the regular solids of *Elements* XIII) with an alleged aesthetic canon. No ancient Greek text found here prescribes the ratio for architecture or art.
- The pentagon and dodecahedron do involve the extreme and mean ratio mathematically. That is a real link between Greek sacred geometry (the cosmic dodecahedron of the *Timaeus*) and φ, but the link is about geometric construction, not architectural proportion.
- Similarly, attributing to "Pythagoras" anything beyond transmigration, a way of life and ritual authority is a traditional interpretation, not an established fact.
