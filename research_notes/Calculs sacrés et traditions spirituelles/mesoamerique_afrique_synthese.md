# Sacred calculation in Mesoamerica (Maya, Aztec) and African divination (Ifá, sikidy, geomancy), with a cross-cultural scholarly assessment

Labels used throughout: **[VERIFIED]** = attested by inscriptions, codices, ethnography or standard reference works; **[TRADITIONAL]** = what the tradition or practitioners hold; **[UNVERIFIABLE/CONTESTED]** = speculative, disputed, or not testable.

Note on method: Firecrawl ran out of credits, so the built-in web search and fetch tools were used. Britannica pages returned HTTP 403 and the NMAI PDF on day meanings also returned 403, so several facts rely on Wikipedia, World History Encyclopedia, university press releases and UNESCO. Wikipedia-sourced items are flagged where they matter.

## Key question 1: What do inscriptions, codices and ethnographies actually attest?

### Takeaway
The Maya Long Count (a mostly base-20 place-value count using a zero placeholder), the 260-day and 365-day counts, the 52-year Calendar Round and the Dresden Codex Venus and eclipse tables are all firmly attested in stone and in codices. Only two known inscriptions mention the 2012 baktun completion, and neither predicts catastrophe. Aztec sources attest the 260/365/52 structure and four New Fire ceremonies (1351 to 1507). For Africa, UNESCO and the ethnography attest Ifá's 256-odu corpus, and the scholarship on sikidy documents a binary/XOR algorithm. Both belong to the family of 16-figure geomancy that the Arabic tradition calls ʿilm al-raml.

### Cited Findings

**Maya numeration and the Long Count [VERIFIED]**
- Long Count units are 1 k'in = 1 day, 1 winal = 20 k'in, 1 tun = 18 winal = 360 days, 1 k'atun = 20 tun = 7,200 days and 1 b'ak'tun = 20 k'atun = 144,000 days. The names of the higher units (piktun, kalabtun, etc.) were coined by modern Mayanists. — [Wikipedia: Mesoamerican Long Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar)
- The count is base 20 except for the second position, which rolls over at 18 (0.0.1.0.0 = 360 days, not 400). The page marks this explanation "citation needed". — [Wikipedia: Long Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar)
- Zero was written with a shell glyph as a placeholder, which counts among the earliest known uses of the zero concept. Other zero glyphs include a hand holding a spiral and a three-lobed flower. — [Wikipedia: Long Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar)
- The base (creation) date is 13.0.0.0.0 4 Ajaw 8 Kumk'u. Under the GMT correlation (JDN 584,283) it falls on 11 August 3114 BCE (proleptic Gregorian). It is written as 13 b'ak'tuns but counts as 0 in calculations. Britannica also gives 4 Ahau 8 Cumku as the base date. — [Wikipedia: Long Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar); [Britannica: Long Count](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Long-Count) (search snippet only; the page returned 403)
- The earliest contemporaneous Long Count date usually cited is Chiapa de Corzo Stela 2, 7.16.3.2.13 = 36 BCE. This is epi-Olmec, not Classic Maya, and an alternative reading gives 182 CE. Takalik Abaj Stela 2 may be earlier but is damaged. — [Wikipedia: Long Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar)
- **Caveat:** the Long Count and its zero were shared across Mesoamerica. They were not exclusively Maya, as the epi-Olmec date shows. — inference from the same source

**Tzolk'in, Haab', Calendar Round [VERIFIED]**
- The Haab' is a 365-day year of 18 months plus 5 unlucky days at year's end. The Calendar Round repeats every 18,980 days, about 52 years. — [Wikipedia: Maya astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_astronomy) (citing Bricker & Bricker, *Astronomy in the Maya Codices*, 2011)
- The 260-day count is called Cholq'ij in K'iche' and Tzolk'in in Yucatec. — [Living in Guatemala guide (secondary)](https://livinginguatemala.com/guides/maya-calendar-cholqij-explained/); [Guatemala Ministry of Culture PDF](https://mcd.gob.gt/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/Armonizacion-Maya.pdf)
- (Arithmetic, not taken from a source: 18 × 20 + 5 = 365; LCM(260, 365) = 18,980 days = 73 tzolk'in = 52 haab'.)

**Dresden Codex Venus and eclipse tables [VERIFIED; interpretation CONTESTED]**
- The Venus Table has an introductory page (24) and five pages of intervals, Calendar Round dates, pictures and auguries, with the last Venus station on page 50. The introduction contains the ring number 6.2.0, Calendar Round 4 Ahaw 8 Kumk'u and the base date 9.9.9.16.0 1 Ahaw 18 K'ayab. — [Fuls, TU Berlin: Venus table](https://user.tu-berlin.de/fuls/Homepage/mayaeng/venus.htm) (cites Schele & Grube 1997)
- The true Venus synodic period is 583.92 days (varying 576.6–588.1). The Maya canonical value was 584 days, so error accumulated. The table covers 65 consecutive Venus cycles (about 104 years) and lists the tzolk'in days of four appearance and disappearance events per cycle. It was reused at least four times with different starting dates between the 10th and 14th centuries AD. — [Wikipedia: Maya astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_astronomy) (citing Bricker & Bricker 2011; Aveni, *Skywatchers*, pp. 184–196 on correction schemes)
- (Arithmetic, not taken from a source: 5 × 584 = 2,920 days = 8 × 365; 65 × 584 = 37,960 days = 146 × 260 = 2 Calendar Rounds = 104 years.)
- An eclipse table on Dresden pp. 51–58 warns of all solar and most lunar eclipses. It covers 405 lunations (about 33 years) and was built to be recycled with corrections. — [Wikipedia: Maya astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_astronomy)
- Gerardo Aldana (UCSB, 2016, *Journal of Astronomy in Culture* 1(1): 57–76) argues that the correction to the Venus calendar was likely developed at Chichén Itzá in the Terminal Classic/Postclassic period, and that the table records observation rather than numerology. This is one scholar's reading and is not settled. — [UCSB News](https://news.ucsb.edu/2016/017062/ancient-mayan-copernicus); [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/maya-calendar-dresden-codex-venus-tables-archaeology-science)
- The Maya associated Venus with war. A Caracol Altar 21 entry reads "star war" against Tikal at 9.6.8.4.2 (27 April 562), and Bricker & Bricker say first morning appearance "was regarded as a time of danger." — [Wikipedia: Maya astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_astronomy)

**The 2012 date: what inscriptions say [VERIFIED]**
- Only two references to the current 13th b'ak'tun completion are known: Tortuguero Monument 6 and La Corona Hieroglyphic Stairway 2, Block V. — [Wikipedia: Long Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar); [UT Austin News, June 2012](https://news.utexas.edu/2012/06/28/maya-scholar-deciphers-meaning-of-newly-discovered-monument-that-refers-to-2012-2/)
- The La Corona text (56 glyphs) commemorates a 696 AD royal visit by Yuknoom Yich'aak K'ahk' of Calakmul. It calls him "13 K'atun lord" (for the k'atun ending of AD 692) and links his reign to the future 13th b'ak'tun. David Stuart said the point was to associate the divine king's time on the throne with time on a cosmic scale and to promote continuity during a time of crisis, not to make a prophecy. — [UT Austin News](https://news.utexas.edu/2012/06/28/maya-scholar-deciphers-meaning-of-newly-discovered-monument-that-refers-to-2012-2/); [UT Austin News, Dec 2012](https://news.utexas.edu/2012/12/17/maya-scholar-debunks-world-ending-myth/)
- Stuart, *The Order of Days: The Maya World and the Truth about 2012* (Harmony/Random House, 2011). — [UT Austin News](https://news.utexas.edu/2012/12/17/maya-scholar-debunks-world-ending-myth/)
- 13.0.0.0.0 fell on 21 December 2012 (GMT); some sources say 23 December depending on correlation. — [Wikipedia: Long Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar); [NASA Sun-Earth Day archive](https://spdf.gsfc.nasa.gov/pub/documents/old/websites/sunearthday.nasa.gov/2012/articles/whatsup_current.php)
- Inscriptions project dates far beyond 2012. Palenque's Temple of the Inscriptions panel reaches the 80th Calendar Round anniversary of Pakal's accession in October 4772 CE. The Wikipedia page gives both 21 and 13 October and is internally inconsistent. — [Wikipedia: Long Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar)
- William Saturno's Xultun (Guatemala) finds include calculations reaching thousands of years into the future. Ricardo Agurcia: the Maya cycle concept is "about rebirth, not death." Tortuguero Monument 6 is mostly about a ruler's life and battles. — [National Geographic, Dec 2012](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/121207-maya-truly-did-not-predict-doomsday-apocalypse)

**Aztec (Mexica) counts [VERIFIED]**
- The tonalpohualli (260 days) pairs the numbers 1–13 with 20 day names. Each 13-day trecena had a patron deity. The xiuhpohualli had 18 "veintenas" of 20 days plus 5 nemontemi days regarded as very unlucky. The two counts realign every 52 years. — [World History Encyclopedia: Aztec Calendar](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/896/the-aztec-calendar/); [Britannica: Aztec calendar](https://britannica.com/topic/Aztec-calendar) (snippet)
- The tonalpouhqui was the day-count specialist ("one who reads/counts days, signs and fates"). The tonalamatl was the divinatory almanac ("book of days"). Codex Borgia plates 1–8 lay out the 260 days in 20 trecenas. — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/896/the-aztec-calendar/); [INALCO: Aztec diviners](https://inalco.fr/en/aztec-diviners-writing-weather)
- The New Fire Ceremony (Binding of the Years, xiuhmolpilli) was held every 52 years to renew the sun. Priests kindled fire on a sacred volcanic hill south-east of Lake Texcoco, and the flame was carried to Tenochtitlan. Ceremonies were held in 1351, 1403, 1455 and 1507. Motecuhzoma II presided over the 1507 one, which his carved throne commemorates. Every second cycle (104 years) carried extra significance. — [World History Encyclopedia: New Fire Ceremony](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/866/the-aztec-new-fire-ceremony/) (essentially one source; primary sources such as Sahagún and Codex Borbonicus were not checked)

**Ifá (Yoruba) [VERIFIED]**
- UNESCO: the Ifá Divination System (Nigeria, ref. 00146) was proclaimed a Masterpiece in 2005 and inscribed on the Representative List in 2008 (3.COM). Its literary corpus, odu, "consists of 256 parts subdivided into verses called ese". The priest is the babalawo, and UNESCO describes the system as using an extensive corpus of texts and mathematical formulas. — [UNESCO ICH](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/ifa-divination-system-00146)
- Structure: 16 principal and 240 derivative odu (16 × 16 = 256), obtained with 16 palm nuts (ikin) or a divining chain (opele). Ikin are considered superior, but both yield the same odu with the same names and rank. — [Encyclopedia.com (Olupona, *Encyclopedia of Religion*, 2005)](https://encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/divination-ifa-divination); search snippets from [SAGE Encyclopedia of African Religion](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/africanreligion/chpt/ikin)
- Procedure: the sign is marked in yellow powder (iyerosun) on the divining tray (opon Ifá) as a "double tetragram". The diviner chants the verses and prescribes sacrifice. — [Encyclopedia.com (Olupona)](https://encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/divination-ifa-divination)
- Ifá is both the practice and the god of divination, also called Ọ̀rúnmìlà. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/divination-ifa-divination)
- William Bascom did fieldwork in Ile-Ifẹ and Yorubaland from 1936 to 1938. His works include *Ifa Divination: Communication between Gods and Men in West Africa* (1969) and *Sixteen Cowries* (1980). — [Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/divination-ifa-divination)
- The diaspora forms are Santería (Cuba), Candomblé (Brazil) and Vodou (Haiti), plus Òrìṣà practice in the US. Ifá texts moved from oral to written form partly for diaspora adherents. The Fon Fa of Benin is a closely related system. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/divination-ifa-divination); [Smithsonian Folklife: Fa divination of Benin](https://festival.si.edu/blog/introduction-to-fa-divination-of-benin)

**Sikidy and ʿilm al-raml [VERIFIED for procedure; origin partly CONTESTED]**
- Arabic geomancy is called ʿilm al-raml ("science of the sand"), also khatt/darb al-raml. It uses 16 four-line figures originally made from random rows of dots in sand. It passed to medieval Europe "virtually unchanged". Ifá uses the same 16 figures under different names, and sikidy is Madagascar's main divination method. — [Wikipedia: Geomancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomancy) (citing Skinner 1980)
- In Europe, Johannes Hartlieb's *Book of All Forbidden Arts* (1456) lists geomancy among the seven forbidden arts. — [Wikipedia: Geomancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomancy) (quoting Láng 2010)
- Sikidy: the diviner (mpisikidy/ombiasy) uses 124 or 200 seeds of fàno (*Entada*) or tamarind. Piles are reduced two at a time until 1 or 2 seeds remain, giving a 4×4 "mother" grid (16 binary values, 65,536 possibilities). Eight more columns are derived by XOR ("same = 2 seeds, different = 1"), giving a 16-column tableau (toetry) with built-in validity checks (parity, identical columns). Marcia Ascher proved these checks in 1997, and Chemillier et al. analysed them in 2007. — [Wikipedia: Sikidy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikidy)
- A 2007 study found mpisikidy recognised figure parity faster than non-diviners. — [Wikipedia: Sikidy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikidy)
- Origin: one view is medieval Arab traders (9th–10th c.), with many Arabic-derived terms (*ombiasy* possibly from Arabic for "prophet"). Another (Ellis & Randrianja) sees an older indigenous art later Islamized. The Antemoro of Matitanana spread it across the island from the 16th century. — [Wikipedia: Sikidy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikidy)
- Wim van Binsbergen argues that Islam was an effective vehicle of reformulation and spread for these geomantic systems in Africa rather than necessarily their sole origin. — [van Binsbergen, Islamic geomancy paper (2003)](https://www.quest-journal.net/shikanda/african_religion/islampaper_def_2003_RTF.pdf) (characterisation from search summary; the paper was not read in full)
- Ron Eglash ("Africa in the Origins of Binary Code"; *African Fractals*, Rutgers UP 1999) describes geomancy as four random binary figures followed by 12 generated by recursively applying the same pairing operation. He notes that Trautmann (1939) first linked the West African Fa/Ifá system to Arabic geomancy. — [Eglash essay (Issuu copy)](https://issuu.com/lukerobertmason/docs/african_divination); [Eglash, *African Fractals* PDF](https://www.monoskop.org/images/f/fc/Eglash_Ron_African_Fractals_Modern_Computing_and_Indigenous_Design.pdf); [OSU press release](https://news.osu.edu/fractals-provide-unusual-theme-in-much-african-culture-and-art/)

### Inferences
- Ifá, Fa, sikidy and Arabic/European geomancy share a 2⁴ = 16 figure base. Ifá pairs two figures (16² = 256), while sikidy and European geomancy generate a 16-figure tableau via XOR-like rules. "Binary structure" is a fair mathematical description, but the practitioners' own concepts are single/double marks or odd/even seeds, not "binary code".
- The Maya Venus table's 104-year span is exactly 2 Calendar Rounds. Its design harmonises astronomy with the ritual counts, which makes it calendrical-astrological and astronomical at once. The "observation vs. numerology" debate (Aldana) is about emphasis, not about whether the table works.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the full list or seniority order of the 16 principal odu (Ogbè, Ọ̀yẹ̀kú, Ìwòrì, Òdí, etc.) from a reliable source. Sources conflict, and Bascom 1969 was not accessible.
- Primary sources on the New Fire (Sahagún's Florentine Codex, Codex Borbonicus) were not consulted.
- The Britannica Maya/Aztec calendar entries returned 403, and the NMAI "Meaning of the Days" PDF returned 403.
- Eglash's stronger claim, a geomancy → Llull → Leibniz binary lineage, is contested (rival I Ching account; Leibniz may have developed binary independently). No authoritative history-of-mathematics source was found to settle it. Treat it as [CONTESTED].
- No reliable source was found for European 12th-century Latin translations of geomancy (e.g., Hugo of Santalla).

## Key question 2: What spiritual and social utility do practitioners describe?

### Takeaway
Practitioners describe these systems as ways to diagnose problems (illness, misfortune, theft), choose auspicious times, reconcile people with gods and ancestors through sacrifice or offering, and preserve moral and literary knowledge. Ancient elites used calendar mathematics to legitimise kingship and tie reigns to cosmic time. The Maya 260-day count is still used by K'iche' and other Maya daykeepers in Guatemala.

### Cited Findings
- **[VERIFIED ethnography]** Barbara Tedlock, *Time and the Highland Maya* (Univ. of New Mexico Press, 1982; rev. ed. 1992; Spanish trans. 2002), studied ritual and cosmology in five K'iche' communities, mainly Momostenango. Her method combined anthropology with formal apprenticeship to a diviner. On returning in 1989 she found observance of the traditional calendar "stronger than ever" despite the civil war. — [UBC Press / UNM listing](https://ubcpress.ca/time-and-the-highland-maya); [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Time-and-the-highland-Maya/oclc/7653289)
- **[VERIFIED]** Guatemala's Ministry of Culture describes the 260-day calendar as one of three Maya calendars still in use, read by spiritual guides called Ajq'ij. Ajq'ij train for 260 days and learn ceremonies and a ceremonial discourse used for divination. The new 260-day cycle begins on 8 B'atz' (Waqxaqib' B'atz'), when new daykeepers are initiated, notably at Santa Cruz del Quiché. — [Guatemala Ministry of Culture PDF](https://mcd.gob.gt/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/Armonizacion-Maya.pdf); [Living in Guatemala (secondary)](https://livinginguatemala.com/guides/maya-calendar-cholqij-explained/); [NMAI resource (search snippet)](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/sites/default/files/resources/The%20Meaning%20of%20the%20Days%20in%20the%20Maya%20Sacred%20Calendar.pdf)
- **[TRADITIONAL]** In 2012 Guatemalan officials said the calendar's end "has nothing to do with the end of the world." For modern Maya the cycle is renewal. — [Calendar in the Sky, UC Berkeley](https://multiverse.ssl.berkeley.edu/Calendar-in-the-Sky/Articles/the-science-of-2012); [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/121207-maya-truly-did-not-predict-doomsday-apocalypse)
- **[VERIFIED, ancient political use]** Stuart reads La Corona as royal propaganda linking a king's reign to cosmic time, meant to "promote continuity during a time of crisis." — [UT Austin News](https://news.utexas.edu/2012/12/17/maya-scholar-debunks-world-ending-myth/)
- **[TRADITIONAL, Aztec]** The New Fire was believed to secure the sun's continuation, and failure would mean the end of the world/civilization. Tonalpouhqui read destinies from day signs (e.g., for naming and auspicious days). — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/866/the-aztec-new-fire-ceremony/); [WHE: Aztec Calendar](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/896/the-aztec-calendar/)
- **[VERIFIED ethnography / TRADITIONAL]** Ifá clients consult on illness, marriage, journeys and royal succession. "No issue is considered too small or complex." The diviner diagnoses the cause and arranges sacrifice, which effects healing. The verses are a major oral genre of myths, proverbs, songs and praise poetry. Training lasts roughly 15–30+ years of memorisation. — [Encyclopedia.com (Olupona)](https://encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/divination-ifa-divination)
- **[VERIFIED]** UNESCO notes that the babalawo interprets signs rather than acting as a spirit medium. It lists threats from ageing practitioners, declining youth interest, limited means of transmission, and past discrimination under colonialism and religious intolerance. — [UNESCO ICH](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/ifa-divination-system-00146)
- **[VERIFIED ethnography]** Sikidy is consulted on illness, journeys, planting, weddings, spouses, lost objects, thieves, sterility and newborns' fates. Remedies are faditra (ritual disposal of an object) and sōrona (offerings). — [Wikipedia: Sikidy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikidy)
- **[Scholarly interpretation]** Chemillier et al. argue that the algorithm's complexity and its faithful oral transmission give the practice authority, even though its predictions cannot be tested experimentally. — [Wikipedia: Sikidy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikidy)

### Inferences
- In all of these systems the calculation is a procedure for legitimate randomisation followed by expert interpretation. The utility practitioners describe is mostly social (counselling, conflict resolution, ritual prescription, preserving the literary corpus) rather than claims about mechanisms.
- The mathematical rigour (XOR checks, Venus/Calendar Round commensuration) works as a guarantee of authority and correctness of procedure. It is not evidence that the outputs are true.

### Gaps
- Direct quotations from contemporary aj q'ij about their purpose were not retrieved (the NMAI PDF was blocked).
- No quantitative data was found on the current number of practising aj q'ij or babalawo.

## Key question 3: What is the scientific consensus on predictive or causal claims?

### Takeaway
No scientific evidence supports the predictive or causal power of numerology or numerical divination. Cognitive science explains the appeal of sacred numbers through over-active pattern detection (apophenia/patternicity), a possible adaptive bias toward false positives, illusory pattern perception under loss of control, and the mathematical fact that small numbers recur everywhere (Guy's "strong law of small numbers"). Dudley's MAA book is the standard mathematician's debunking. None of this negates the documented astronomical precision of Maya tables or the cultural value of these traditions.

### Cited Findings
- **2012 [VERIFIED consensus]:** NASA/JPL stated there was no threat. "Nibiru does not exist" (David Morrison, NASA), and the calendar "does not end on December 21, 2012; it's just the end of the cycle" (Don Yeomans, JPL). No Maya codex or inscription mentions doomsday. — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/121207-maya-truly-did-not-predict-doomsday-apocalypse); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/nasa-crushes-2012-mayan-apocalypse-claims); [UC Berkeley Calendar in the Sky](https://multiverse.ssl.berkeley.edu/Calendar-in-the-Sky/Articles/the-science-of-2012)
- Scholars disagree on exactly how the Maya conceived the cycle's end, but many see it as a new beginning. — [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/17572-doomsday-world-2012.html)
- **Dudley:** Underwood Dudley, *Numerology: Or What Pythagoras Wrought* (Mathematical Association of America, Spectrum series; ISBN 0-88385-524-0). The MAA lists it as 1997, while the Mathematical Gazette review lists it as 1998 with 316 pp. It examines and exposes fallacies in Pythagorean number mysticism, gematria, 666, pyramid-inch theories, biorhythms and modern name/birthdate numerology. Reviewed positively by Walter S. Sizer (MAA Reviews, 1999) and in *Choice*. — [MAA Reviews](https://old.maa.org/node/108433); [Mathematical Gazette review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/mathematical-gazette/article/numerology-or-what-pythagoras-wrought-by-underwood-dudley-pp-316-1995-1998-isbn-0-88385-524-0-mathematical-association-of-america/2F68B96D63C6661AFC58FC46DC5D5F6D); [Skeptics NZ review](https://articles.skeptics.nz/2000/05/01/numerology-or-what-pythagoras-wrought)
- **Law of small numbers (two distinct concepts):**
  - Tversky & Kahneman, "Belief in the Law of Small Numbers," *Psychological Bulletin* 76(2): 105–110 (1971). It describes the exaggerated belief that small samples represent the population, a cognitive bias. — [CAUSEweb](https://www.causeweb.org/cause/node/5464); [Cambridge reprint, *Judgment under Uncertainty* 1982](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/judgment-under-uncertainty/belief-in-the-law-of-small-numbers/BC22F08226B4BC58280BCCBA5DC9C312)
  - Richard K. Guy, "The Strong Law of Small Numbers," *American Mathematical Monthly* 95(8): 697–712 (1988): "there aren't enough small numbers to meet the many demands made of them." It explains why the same small numbers (3, 7, 12, 13, 16, 20…) produce many striking coincidences. It won the MAA Lester R. Ford Award. — [Wikipedia: Strong law of small numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_law_of_small_numbers); [MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/StrongLawofSmallNumbers.html)
- **Apophenia / patternicity:** Michael Shermer (Scientific American, Dec 2008) coined "patternicity", the tendency to find meaningful patterns in meaningless noise. He later added "agenticity" (*The Believing Brain*, 2011). — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/patternicity-finding-meaningful-patterns/); [Shermer site](https://michaelshermer.com/2008/12/patternicity/)
- Foster & Kokko (2009, *Proc. R. Soc. B*) model why natural selection can favour strategies "that make many incorrect causal associations in order to establish those that are essential for survival", which would make superstition a by-product of adaptive error management. — [Foster & Kokko PDF](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/foster_kokko.pdf)
- Whitson & Galinsky (*Science*, 2008): participants who lacked control perceived more illusory patterns, including superstitions and conspiracies. — [PubMed 18832647](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18832647/)
- Apophenia's ultimate cause is not confirmed, so evolutionary accounts remain hypotheses. — [Wikipedia: Apophenia](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Apophany)
- **Numerology's status:** it is classified as pseudoscience, with no credible evidence that numbers derived from names or birthdates predict personality, health or events. Secondary sources only. — [Media Bias/Fact Check pseudoscience dictionary](https://mediabiasfactcheck.com/pseudoscience-dictionary-numerology/); [Skeptoid #626](https://skeptoid.com/episodes/626)
- A 2017 paper in the *Journal of Articles in Support of the Null Hypothesis* (vol. 13, no. 2) tested numerological birth numbers against Nobel laureates 1901–2010. Its exact conclusion was not verified, but the journal's scope implies a null result. — [JASNH PDF](https://www.jasnh.com/pdf/Vol13-No2-article2.pdf)
- Chemillier et al. state that sikidy's predictions cannot be tested experimentally. — [Wikipedia: Sikidy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikidy)

### Inferences
- The overall picture separates three things:
  - **Verified:** Maya and Mesoamerican calendar mathematics and astronomical tables were real, sophisticated, observation-based achievements. Ifá and sikidy embody genuine combinatorial and Boolean structures.
  - **Traditional:** the claims that day signs shape destiny, that odu reveal divine will, and that the New Fire renewed the sun are religious meanings.
  - **Unverifiable/unsupported:** any claim that these numbers have causal or predictive power over events. No controlled evidence supports this, and the 2012 case shows modern projections can even contradict the source texts.
- The persistence of sacred numbers across unrelated cultures is well explained by shared cognition (finger-based counting likely underlies base 20, though I found no source for this; see Gaps), the recurrence of small numbers (Guy), pattern-seeking biases (Shermer; Foster & Kokko), and anxiety reduction (Whitson & Galinsky). None of these requires the numbers to have intrinsic power.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed meta-analysis specifically testing numerology. The evidence of absence is mostly a consensus position in skeptical and secondary literature.
- I found no source for the common claim that vigesimal counting derives from fingers plus toes.
- Anthony Aveni's and Michael Coe's own statements on 2012 and on numerology were not retrieved directly. Aveni is cited only via Wikipedia (*Skywatchers*), and Coe's *The Maya* / *Breaking the Maya Code* were not consulted.
- I found no controlled empirical study of the accuracy of Ifá or sikidy diagnoses.
