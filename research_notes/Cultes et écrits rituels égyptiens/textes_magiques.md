## 1. Medical-magical papyri (Ebers, Edwin Smith, Hearst, London, Berlin, Brooklyn, Kahun)

### Takeaway
Egyptian medical papyri are recipe compilations that mix prescriptions with incantations. The Edwin Smith Papyrus is the exception: its recto is an organised surgical case-book, but its verso holds eight incantations against "the pest of the year". Ritner argues that "medicine" and "magic" were practised by the same priest-trained specialists: the doctor (swnw), the priest of Sakhmet (wab Sekhmet) and the amulet-man (sau).

### Cited Findings
**General**
- [V] Breasted's 1930 table gives the manuscript sizes and dates:
  - Papyrus Ebers: 110 columns, 2,289 lines, 877 recipes, "Early Sixteenth Cent. B.C."
  - Papyrus Hearst: 16 columns plus fragments, 273 lines, 260 recipes, 16th century BC.
  - Berlin Papyrus: 24 columns, 279 lines, 204 recipes, 16th century BC.
  - London Papyrus: 19 very fragmentary columns, 253 lines, 63 recipes, 11th century BC.
  - Breasted adds: "The dates given are those of the manuscripts. The materials themselves are obviously older."
  - Source: [Breasted 1930, I, p. 2 n. 1](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/oip3.pdf).
  - These dates are Breasted's (1930). Modern chronology may differ, for example Ebers c. 1550 BC under Amenhotep I [U].
- [V] The medical rolls attribute themselves to divine origins. Breasted, *Edwin Smith* I, pp. 2–3, quotes two examples:
  - The Berlin Medical Papyrus was "found in ancient writings in a chest containing documents under the feet of Anubis in Letopolis" in the time of King Usephais. Its maker was "the scribe of sacred writing, the chief of the excellent physicians, Neterhotep".
  - The London Medical Papyrus: "This book was found in the night, having fallen into the court of the temple in ⌜Khemmis⌝ … by the hand of the lector of this temple." — [Breasted 1930, I, pp. 2–3](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/oip3.pdf)
- [V] Zinn notes that P. Ebers treats heka as something to be driven out of patients' bodies. She says physicians, as priests of Sekhmet, were trained in the House of Life in both magic and medicine (citing Allen 2005) — [Zinn 2013](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf)

**Papyrus Ebers** (Leipzig University Library; manuscript early 18th Dynasty)
- [V] Content: 877 recipes (Breasted's count, above).
- [V] Excerpt, Ebers §854 (col. 99/2–3), quoted from Wreszinski 1913 in Ritner 1993, p. 53. It is useful for practitioners: "…when any doctor, priest of Sakhmet or amulet-man places his hands or fingers on any head, on any back of the head, on any hands, on any chest ("place of the heart") … then he places them on the heart, for its vessels belong to all its limbs." — [Ritner 1993, p. 53](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Excerpt of a magical recipe, Ebers §385, as quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 51 n. 240: "Come malachite! Come malachite! Come vigor! Come vigor! … To be recited over malachite pounded up with honey." — [Ritner 1993, p. 51 n. 240](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Ebers §733 (col. 88/13) is a "Prescription to expel the craft of amulets", a remedy against hostile amulet-magic — [Ritner 1993, p. 53 n. 246](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

**Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus** (New York Academy of Medicine; manuscript c. 17th–16th century BC per Breasted)
- [V] Structure: 21½ columns in all. The recto has 17 columns (377 lines). The verso has 4½ columns (92 lines) of incantations and recipes — [Breasted 1930, I, p. 3](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/oip3.pdf)
- [V] The recto has 48 cases. Each closes with one of three verdicts, which Breasted (I, p. 46) translates as: "An ailment which I will treat." / "An ailment with which I will contend." / "An ailment not to be treated." — [Breasted 1930, I, p. 46](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/oip3.pdf)
- [V] The verso has eight incantations against "the pest of the year" (XVIII 1–XX 12), plus five recipes. The rituals are prescribed:
  - the man protected by the 1st incantation wears two vulture feathers;
  - the 2nd incantation is spoken while carrying des-wood around the house;
  - the 5th is written on a linen band worn at the neck;
  - the 7th uses a nefret-flower tied with linen to des-wood, passed over food and bed.
  - Source: [Breasted 1930, I, pp. 472–473](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/oip3.pdf)
- [V] Excerpt, title of the first incantation, XVIII 1: "Incantation for exorcising the wind of the pest of the year." Excerpt, first incantation: "O Flame-in-His-Face! Presider over the horizon, speak thou to the chief of the Hemesut-house, who makes Osiris, first of the land, to flourish." — [Breasted 1930, I, pp. 473–474](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/oip3.pdf)
- [R] Breasted conjectures that the same owner might have held both the Smith and Ebers papyri and that they were found together. He presents this as possible, not proven — [Breasted 1930, I, p. 471](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/oip3.pdf)

**Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus** (UCL Petrie Museum UC 32057; El-Lahun, found by Petrie in 1889; 12th Dynasty, c. 1825 BC per secondary sources)
- [V] It has three pages, recto only, of 29, 30 and 28 lines. Part one covers aches and pains with their treatment; part two mostly concerns conception — [UCL collections record](https://collections.ucl.ac.uk/Details/petrie/40186) (via search summary)
- [V] Edition: F. Ll. Griffith, *Hieratic Papyri from Kahun and Gurob* (1898), pls. V–VI and pp. 5–14, as cited by Breasted — [Breasted 1930, I, p. 2](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/oip3.pdf). Breasted dates the Kahun medical documents to "perhaps as old as about 2000 B.C."

**Brooklyn snake papyrus** (Brooklyn Museum 47.218.48a–f + 47.218.85a–f; Late Period copy, 4th century BC per the Anthropozoologica title)
- [V] The roll was split while still rolled: the upper half became .48 and the lower half .85. It is not on display — [search summary of Brooklyn Museum records](https://brooklynmuseum.org/opencollection/print/object/60690)
- [V] Editions and translations:
  - Sauneron, *Un traité égyptien d'ophiologie* (Cairo, IFAO 1989), French;
  - M. Q. Stewart 2020 (CUNY working paper), English, which claims to be "the first full English translation" — [CUNY](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/bc_pubs/277);
  - Golding 2020 (UNISA thesis), English — [UNISA](https://ir.unisa.ac.za/handle/10500/26760?show=full);
  - Sanchez, Meltzer et al. 2024 (Lockwood Press).
- [V] Ritner cites P. Brooklyn 47.218.48+85 from Sauneron 1989 — [Ritner 1993, p. 80 n. 369](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

**Hearst, London (BM EA 10059), Berlin (P. Berlin 3038)**
- [V] Sizes and dates come from Breasted's table only (above). Ritner cites P. Hearst cols. 5/10 ff. (Reisner 1905) — [Ritner 1993](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

### Inferences
- The same rolls hold prescriptions, incantations and rubrics on how to perform them, for example over what to recite. "Rational" medicine and "magic" were therefore one practice, not two.
- The divine-origin colophons legitimate the texts as temple knowledge. The "lector of this temple" in the London papyrus colophon points to priestly custody.

### Gaps
- I read no translation of Hearst, Berlin 3038, London BM 10059, Kahun or Brooklyn, so there are no direct excerpts. The Brooklyn translations by Stewart and Golding are online, but the CUNY PDF was behind a Cloudflare challenge.
- I did not open the Ebbell 1937 or Bryan Ebers translations. Ebers excerpts reach me only via Ritner.
- Exact modern dates and museum inventory numbers for Ebers, Hearst, Berlin and London are not verified from museum pages here. The common ones are Hearst at UC Berkeley Bancroft/Hearst Museum, London BM EA 10059 and Berlin P. 3038 [U].

## 2. Execration texts and the "breaking of the red pots"

### Takeaway
Middle Kingdom execration deposits are inscribed red pots and prisoner figurines listing foreign and Egyptian enemies. They were broken and/or buried, and they continue an Old Kingdom funerary rite of "breaking the red vases" (sd dšr.w, Pyramid Texts spell 244). Ritner's relative chronology runs: Cairo alabaster figurines (Senwosret I) → Mirgissa (Amenemhat II–Senwosret II) → Berlin bowls (mid 12th Dyn.) → Saqqara figures (end of 12th Dyn.).

### Cited Findings
- [V] Chronological sequence of the Middle Kingdom assemblages, after Posener 1987 and Koenig 1990: "the Cairo alabaster figurines (Sesostris I), the Mirgissa deposit (Amenemhat II–Sesostris II), the Berlin bowls (mid Twelfth Dynasty), and the Saqqara figures (end of Twelfth Dynasty)". The Cairo alabaster figures are JdE 63955–59, possibly from Helwan — [Ritner 1993, p. 140 nn. 620–621](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] The formula, summarised by Ritner: the pots and figurines (of clay, limestone, alabaster, wood or wax) list Nubian, Asiatic and Libyan princes, then hostile Egyptians, then evil forces such as "evil speech, slander, plots, strife, nightmares". Excerpt of the "rebellion formula" from the Berlin bowls, after Sethe 1926, quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 140: "who may rebel, who may plot, who may fight, who may think of fighting, or who may think of rebelling on this entire earth." — [Ritner 1993, p. 140](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Berlin bowls: purchased from a Luxor dealer, without context. They are 80 inscribed red vessels and the first exemplars of the genre to be discovered. Edition: Sethe 1926 — [Ritner 1993, pp. 144, 153](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Mirgissa (2nd-cataract fortress, Nubia), excavated by Vila and published by Koenig 1990. It consists of four interments with:
  - 197 broken inscribed red vases (and ostraca);
  - 437 broken uninscribed red vases;
  - 346 mud figures;
  - 3 limestone prisoner figurines plus the head of a fourth;
  - "the remains of a human sacrifice".
  - The site lay about 600 m from the fortress, buried in sand — [Ritner 1993, pp. 153–155](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [R] Ritner's interpretation is that the burial in sand is religious (sand is linked to creation and purification), not merely practical. This contradicts Vila's practical explanation — [Ritner 1993, p. 155](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] PT spell 244 (§249), translated by Faulkner 1969 and quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 144: "Hail, [Osiris NN,] this is the Eye of Horus. Take it so that you may be strong and that he may be terrified of you—Break the red vases." — [Ritner 1993, p. 144](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Coffin Texts spell 926, translated by Faulkner 1978 and quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 145: "Wash yourself, sit down at the meal, put your hands on it; divert the god's offerings, break the red pots, give cold water, purify the offering-tables …" — [Ritner 1993, p. 145](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Ritual context: the rite is part of the funerary offering liturgy. It involves the "Bearers of the Red Pots" (ḥry.w dšr.w), and in Old Kingdom tombs (Meir, Mereruka at Saqqara) it is paired with "removing the footprints". Ritner suggests the pots were those used in the offering meal and broken at its end — [Ritner 1993, pp. 145–146](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Later echoes:
  - The Saite fortress of Defenna has 30–40 limestone prisoner figurines — [Ritner 1993, p. 154](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
  - The Harem Conspiracy against Ramesses III involved wax figures. P. Rollin, after Goedicke 1963, as quoted in Zinn: "writings were made for enchanting, for banishing, for confusing – because some 'gods' were made into wax and some men (also)" — [Zinn 2013](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf)

### Inferences
- The execration rite was a state ritual. It was performed at Nubian fortresses, and Ritner compares the deposit to a foundation deposit. Its actors were presumably priests and lector priests, though the deposits themselves do not name the officiants.

### Gaps
- Saqqara figurines: I found no detail beyond the date (end of 12th Dynasty). Their excavator and edition (Posener 1987 and others) were not verified.
- The Mirgissa "human sacrifice" comes from Ritner, following Vila. Its interpretation is debated in the literature, and I did not verify the counter-arguments.

## 3. Magical papyri: Harris, Chester Beatty, Leiden I 348, Turin, Demotic London-Leiden, PGM

### Takeaway
New Kingdom hieratic magical rolls hold spells against crocodiles, snakes, demons and disease. They were owned by temple personnel and by literate scribes at Deir el-Medina. The Roman-period Demotic and Greek papyri from Thebes (Anastasi collection) carry on Egyptian ritual techniques, including lamp and vessel divination with a boy medium.

### Cited Findings
**Harris Magical Papyrus** (BM EA 10042; New Kingdom; edition by H. O. Lange 1927)
- [V] Excerpt from col. 6/10, translated by Lange 1927 and quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 203: "First spell of enchanting all that is in the water, concerning which the chief lector priests say: 'Do not reveal it to others.' A veritable secret of the House of Life." — [Ritner 1993, p. 203](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Ritner notes that the completed amulet could be given to "a man at the prow of the boat", so the practitioner keeps the spell and the user receives only the object — [Ritner 1993, p. 206 n. 956 (page approximate)](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Ritner also uses the Harris papyrus as an example that "magical" texts appear in "religious" contexts — [Ritner 1993](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

**Chester Beatty magical papyri** (BM; Deir el-Medina, Ramesside; edition by Gardiner 1935, *Hieratic Papyri in the BM, 3rd series*)
- [V] Ownership: the "Dream Book" and other Chester Beatty magical rolls belonged to the scribes Amennakhte and Qenherkhepeshef. Qenherkhepeshef copied spells against demons and ghosts. P. Chester Beatty 8 says it was a temple text, "This writing was found in the library, (in a) room of the temple" (col. 4/3). A spell of vo. 7/7 is "to be recited by the chief lector priest" — [Ritner 1993, p. 206 n. 954](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Techniques:
  - P. Chester Beatty 7, col. 5/5: a red snake pierced by black knives;
  - P. Chester Beatty 8, col. 4/10: reversal of enemy names;
  - P. Chester Beatty 8, cols. 4/5–5/3: burials of enemies.
  - Source: [Ritner 1993](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

**Leiden I 348** (Rijksmuseum van Oudheden; Ramesside)
- [V] The only verified detail: Ritner cites col. 13/2–3 as a ritual-washing example in which honey is poured into a bowl — [Ritner 1993, n. 377](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf). I read no translation (the edition is Borghouts 1971) and give no excerpt.

**Turin papyrus: "Isis and the secret name of Ra"** (Turin, Pleyte & Rossi pls. 31, 77, 131–138; Ramesside)
- [V] Budge gives the Turin source as "Papyrus de Turin, pl. 31, 77, 131-138", published by Pleyte and Rossi — [Budge 1912, Introduction](https://archive.org/details/egyptianliteratu0001eawa)
- [V] Excerpt of the narrative (Budge 1912, chapter opening, c. p. 42): "Behold, the goddess Isis lived in the form of a woman, who had the knowledge of words [of power]." — [Budge 1912, *Legends of the Gods*, opening of "The Legend of Ra and Isis", c. p. 42 (page approximate from OCR)](https://archive.org/details/egyptianliteratu0001eawa)
- [V] Excerpt of the operative spell and rubric (p. 55): "Let Ra live, and let the poison die; and if the poison live then Ra shall die. And similarly, a certain man, the son of a certain man, shall live and the poison shall die." — [Budge 1912, p. 55](https://archive.org/details/egyptianliteratu0001eawa)
- [V] Ritual use, from the rubric as Budge renders it: "The above words shall be said over an image of Temu and an image of Heru-Hekennu, and over an image of Isis and an image of Horus." Budge's introduction adds that the words were written on papyrus or linen, steeped in water and drunk against snake-bite — [Budge 1912, pp. 55 and Introduction](https://archive.org/details/egyptianliteratu0001eawa)
- [R] The myth is a historiola. It serves as the precedent for a cure against scorpion and snake poison: "NN son of NN" replaces Ra.

**Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden** (BM EA 10070 + Leiden I 383; Thebes; 3rd century AD)
- [V] It was found at Thebes with mostly Greek papyri of similar subject matter and bought by Anastasi, the Swedish consul at Alexandria. It was already torn into two parts. Reuvens and Leemans dated it to the first half of the 3rd century AD — [Griffith & Thompson 1904, I, "History of the MS." and "Date"](https://archive.org/details/the-demotic-magical-papyrus-of-london-and-leiden-v.-1)
- [V] Excerpt, col. VI/1–4 ("An inquiry of the lamp"): "You go to a clean dark cell without light and you dig a new hole in an east wall and you take a white lamp in which no minium or gum water has been put, its wick being clean…" — [Griffith & Thompson 1904, I, col. VI](https://archive.org/details/the-demotic-magical-papyrus-of-london-and-leiden-v.-1)
- [V] Excerpt, col. VI/5–6 (the boy medium): "You put your finger on the boy's head, his eyes being closed. When you have finished you make him open his eyes towards the lamp; then he sees the shadow of the god about the lamp" — [Griffith & Thompson 1904, I, col. VI](https://archive.org/details/the-demotic-magical-papyrus-of-london-and-leiden-v.-1)
- [V] Other contents: a vessel-inquiry of Chons (Khonsu), and a scorpion cure in col. 20/1–27, which Ritner notes keeps the "old methodology" — [Griffith & Thompson 1904](https://archive.org/details/the-demotic-magical-papyrus-of-london-and-leiden-v.-1); [Ritner 1993](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

**Greek Magical Papyri (PGM)**
- [V] Ritner shows PGM continuity with Egyptian technique. For example, PGM XIII (= P. Leiden I 395) uses the "traditional methodology", and PGM IV 332 binding is compared with Egyptian knots. He cites Preisendanz 1928 and Betz 1986 — [Ritner 1993, pp. 143 n. 640 and §PGM XIII](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Harris rubric and the Chester Beatty colophons show that "private" spells were temple knowledge (House of Life) that literate scribes copied for their own use.

### Gaps
- I read no translation of the Chester Beatty magical spells (beyond the dream book), of Leiden I 348 or of the PGM (Betz). There are no direct excerpts for these.
- The museum numbers BM EA 10042 (Harris), BM EA 10070 / Leiden I 383 (Demotic London-Leiden) and the Turin inventory number (often cited as Turin CGT 54051 / P. Turin 1993) are not verified from museum pages in this session [U].

## 4. Divination: lucky/unlucky day calendars, Dream Book, oracles, oracular amuletic decrees

### Takeaway
New Kingdom hemerologies (Cairo Calendar 86637) and the Dream Book (P. Chester Beatty III = BM EA 10683) classify days and dreams as "good" or "bad". Bad entries are written in red ink. Third Intermediate Period oracular amuletic decrees were written oracles of the gods, issued by temples and worn rolled in cylindrical cases.

### Cited Findings
**Cairo Calendar (JdE 86637)**
- [V] The Egyptian Museum bought it from a dealer in 1943. Bakir published it in 1966 as *The Cairo Calendar No. 86637*. It is described as the best-preserved Calendar of Lucky and Unlucky Days. Dates proposed in secondary sources range from 1271 to 1163 BC. Lucky days are written in black and unlucky days in red. Translations: Bakir 1966 (English) and Leitz 1994, *Tagewählerei* (German) — [Porceddu/Jetsu et al.](https://www.mv.helsinki.fi/home/jetsu/papers/egypt1.pdf); [arXiv 1601.06990](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1601.06990); [ANEFEST](https://anefest.spbu.ru/en/articles/ancient-minor-asia/141-calendar-of-lucky-and-unlucky-days-egypt.html) (via search summaries)
- [R] The Helsinki group claims a 2.85-day periodicity in the lucky prognoses and links it to the variable star Algol. This is a contested astrophysical interpretation, not an Egyptological consensus — [arXiv 1601.06990](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1601.06990)
- [V] Ritner criticises scholars for lumping "calendars of lucky and unlucky days, dreams, horoscopes" under "magic" — [Ritner 1993, ch. 1 "Toward a definition of magic" (exact page not verified)](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

**Dream Book (P. Chester Beatty III, BM EA 10683)**
- [V] The recto is the hieratic dream book. The verso, copied by the scribe Qenherkhepeshef, has the Kadesh poem. Each entry has the form "if a man sees himself in a dream…" followed by good/bad and an interpretation, with bad dreams in red. Later owners were Khaemnun and Amennakht. The BM date is c. 1220 BC, and the language may be Middle Kingdom. Edition: Gardiner 1935 — [British Museum EA 10683](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10683-1) (via search summary)
- [V] Excerpt, col. 7/18, from Gardiner 1935, I, p. 16, as quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 21: "(If a man see himself in a dream) being bitten by a dog—BAD; (it means) a cleaving fast to him of magic" — [Ritner 1993, p. 21 n. 82](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Excerpt, col. 10/9, from Gardiner 1935, I, p. 19, as quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 151: "(If a man see himself in a dream) breaking a vessel with his feet—BAD; it means fighting." — [Ritner 1993, p. 151](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

**Oracles and oracular amuletic decrees**
- [V] The Egyptian term for oracle is "miracle" (bjȝjt), and the procedure is the "petitioning of the god" (pḥ-nṯr) during festivals. The divine decrees were written down, rolled up, and could be worn as amulets — [Zinn 2013](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf)
- [V] Excerpt from an oracular amuletic decree, P. BM 10587, ll. 90–98 (22nd or 23rd Dynasty; edition I. E. S. Edwards 1960, *Oracular Amuletic Decrees of the Late New Kingdom*), quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 216. Thoth speaks: "I shall nullify every evil pḥ-nṯr, every evil upset(?), and every evil thing which has been done to NN the son of NN, … I shall make them recoil on those who did them" — [Ritner 1993, p. 216 and n. 1004](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] "Written oracles were worn in cylindrical cases as phylacteries" (Edwards 1960, I, pp. xviii–xix) — [Ritner 1993, p. 216 n. 1004](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Ritner notes that the same oracular rite could be used for hostile ends ("an evil petitioning of god"). The decrees protected against this — [Ritner 1993, p. 216](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

### Inferences
- The red/black ink coding in both the Calendar and the Dream Book probably reflects the same scribal convention, in which red marks danger or Sethian elements.

### Gaps
- Ticket oracles (written yes/no questions put to the god, often ostraca from Deir el-Medina): I found no source in this session.
- I read no Bakir translation, so there is no Cairo Calendar excerpt.

## 5. Protective objects with texts: cippi / Metternich Stela, magic wands, amulets, hypocephali

### Takeaway
Cippi of Horus-on-the-crocodiles were healing monuments. Water poured over their texts was drunk by snake- or scorpion-bite victims. The Metternich Stela (Met 50.85, Nectanebo II) is the fullest example. Apotropaic ivory "wands" and serpent wands appear in the Middle Kingdom magician's tomb at the Ramesseum.

### Cited Findings
**Metternich Stela** (Metropolitan Museum 50.85; 30th Dynasty, Nectanebo II, c. 360–343 BC)
- [V] It is a magical stela (Cippus of Horus) of the Late Period, accession 50.85, Fletcher Fund 1950. Water poured over it was collected and drunk by the patient. The myth has Thoth, sent from the sun boat, cure the bitten infant Horus — [Met Museum](https://metmuseum.org/toah/works-of-art/50.85) (via search summary; my direct fetch returned HTTP 429)
- [V] It was presented to Prince Metternich in 1828 and was at Kynžvart until 1950 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metternich_Stela) [lower authority]
- [V] Its origin was a temple: it was copied by the god's father Nestem "after he had found it removed from the domain of Osiris Mnevis" (Borghouts 1978, §95) — [Ritner 1993, p. 206 n. 953](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Excerpt, Metternich Stela (Isis speaking), translated by Ritner 1993, p. 34: "I am Isis the goddess, the possessor of magic, who performs magic, effective of speech, excellent of words." — [Ritner 1993, p. 34](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Excerpt from the formula common to Horus cippi (against lions, crocodiles, snakes and scorpions), quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 151: "Make them for me as the pebbles of the desert, as the potsherds along the street." — [Ritner 1993, p. 151](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Other editions: N. E. Scott 1951 (*MMA Bulletin*); C. E. Sander-Hansen 1956; a German translation by P. Dils on TLA/SAE — [SAE Leipzig](https://sae.saw-leipzig.de/en/documents/metternich-stela?version=31)

**Magic wands and serpent wands**
- [V] The Ramesseum magician's box (late Middle Kingdom, reused 12th Dynasty tomb shaft under the Ramesseum storerooms) measured about 18×12×12 in. It contained:
  - 23 fragmentary papyri and a bundle of reed pens;
  - four broken ivory "apotropaic knives";
  - four female dolls, a Beset statuette holding snakes, and a bronze uraeus "entangled in a mass of hair";
  - an ivory herdsman with a calf.
  - A jackal was drawn on the lid, which reads as the title "overseer of secrets" (ḥry-sštȝ). Apart from two literary works, the papyri are ritual or medico-magical — [Ritner 1993, pp. 222–223](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf); [Zinn 2013](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf)
- [V] Serpent wands survive in bronze. Fitzwilliam E.63.1896 comes from that Ramesseum tomb, and BM EA 52831 was found wrapped in a mummy shroud near Deir el-Bahari. Heka holds serpent wands in the Greenfield Papyrus (BM EA 10554/88) — [Zinn 2013](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf); see Ritner 2006, "And each staff transformed into a snake"

**Amulets**
- [V] Amulet terminology: sȝ means "protection/amulet", wḏȝ means "health/amulet", and the "papyrus column" amulet (wȝḏ) is made of green stone. Ritner's Table 2 lists named amulets — [Ritner 1993, pp. 49–52](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Book of the Dead spell 160, Allen 1974, quoted in Ritner 1993, p. 51 n. 240: "Spell for the papyrus amulet of green feldspar … If it stays sound then I stay sound." — [Ritner 1993, p. 51 n. 240](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Magic bricks (BD 151): the set of Henutmehyt, BM EA 41544–41547 — [Zinn 2013](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf)

**Hypocephali**
- [V] Ritner mentions only a bowl misinterpreted as a terracotta hypocephalus — [Ritner 1993, p. 64 n.](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)

### Inferences
- Cippi turn the execration idea (enemies as "potsherds") into a popular healing device. Text and water work together: drinking the text's power.

### Gaps
- Hypocephali (Late/Ptolemaic disks placed under the mummy's head, based on BD 162): I found no source in this session. There are no verified facts or quotes.
- Middle Kingdom apotropaic ivories: I verified no museum page with inscriptions such as "protection by day and night". Altenmüller's study was not consulted.

## 6. Practitioners: lector priest (ḫry-ḥb), scorpion charmer (ḫrp Srqt), amulet-man (sȝw), House of Life (pr-ꜥnḫ)

### Takeaway
Ritner argues that Egyptian magicians were priests, and that "itinerant magicians did not exist". The chief lector priest (ḫry-ḥb ḥry-tp) and the "scribe of the House of Life" were the archetypal magicians. Scorpion charmers and amulet-men were specialised healers, trained in the House of Life and often also physicians.

### Cited Findings
- [V] Excerpt: "The direct priestly affiliation of magicians is highly significant, and strongly suggests that itinerant magicians did not exist in ancient Egypt. Even the most likely candidate, the ḫrp Srḳt or 'scorpion charmer,' was undoubtedly schooled in the House of Life, and was not infrequently a trained physician (swnw)." — [Ritner 1993, p. 222](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf). This is Ritner's thesis and should be labelled [R] as an interpretation.
- [V] The magician is designated "chief lector priest" or "scribe of the House of Life". The Egyptian ḥry-tp lies behind the Hebrew ḥarṭummîm of the Joseph story (Gen 41:8, 24), rendered in Greek as "interpreters (of dreams)" and in Coptic as "scribe of the House of Life" — [Ritner 1993, p. 222 and n. 1032](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] The magicians of P. Westcar (Webaoner, Djadjaemankh) are chief lector priests. Djedi is a commoner but has books and students — [Ritner 1993, n. 1031](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] The amulet-man (sȝw) means "protector, guardian, amulet-man". The court title sȝ-bity means "amulet-man of the King of Lower Egypt". The sȝw-Srqt, "amulet-man of Selket" (apparently = ḫrp-Srqt, "controller of Selket"), accompanied mining expeditions to treat scorpion stings (Gardiner 1917) — [Ritner 1993, pp. 51–53 and n. 244](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Temple priests acted as magicians while off-duty from their rotation, so the private magician and the cultic priest were the same person — [Zinn 2013, citing Ritner 1992](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf)
- [V] House of Life:
  - Foreigners were excluded. P. Salt 825, col. 7/5: "An Asiatic may not enter into this House of Life, he may not see it." — [Ritner 1993, p. 203 n. 941](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
  - Secrecy rubrics warn against revealing the texts, e.g. "Roll for understanding the words of the House of Life … to be used … without the rabble's knowledge" — [Ritner 1993, p. 203](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] Royal jubilee rituals show "courtiers in charge of magic", "supervisors of magic", scribes of the House of Life and a chief lector priest — [Ritner 1993, p. 53 n. 243](https://isac-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/isac-publications/saoc54_4th.pdf)
- [V] The "prophet of Heka" (ḥm-nṯr Ḥkȝ) is attested, and there was a "Mansion of Heka" at Heliopolis and in the Memphite necropolis — [Zinn 2013](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf)
- [V] Magical techniques listed by Ritner 1993, pp. 1–2: circumambulation, spitting, licking, swallowing, images, trampling, binding, breaking, burning, red colour, sand, piercing, burial, oracular consultation — as summarised in [Zinn 2013](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/570/1/Magic%2C%20Pharaonic%20Egypt.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Ramesseum box, with its jackal sign "overseer of secrets", gives the archaeological link between a lector-priest-type owner, magical papyri and wands.

### Gaps
- Von Känel 1984, *Les prêtres-ouâb de Sekhmet et les conjurateurs de Serket*, is the key study on scorpion charmers. It is cited by Ritner but I did not consult it.
- There are no UCLA Encyclopedia entries, and I read no Pinch or Borghouts.
