## Edfu: inventory of attested festivals and rituals (Beautiful Meeting / Feast of Behdet, Coronation of the Falcon, Victory / Triumph of Horus, New Year, foundation, daily ritual)

### Takeaway
The Ptolemaic Edfu temple walls describe at least four major Horus festivals in detail, plus the daily ritual and the foundation and consecration rites. The four festivals are the New Year (wp-rnpt), the Beautiful Feast of Behdet (sHn nfr n BHdt, month Epiphi), the Coronation of the Sacred Falcon (xaw nswt, first days of Tybi) and the Festival of Victory (Hb qnt, with the "Triumph of Horus" drama). Eleven temple calendars at Edfu, Kom Ombo, Dendera and Esna give the names, dates and lengths of many more festivals, but say little about how they were performed.

### Cited Findings

**General framework (Coppens, UEE)**
- Daily ritual and festivals share one core: "seeing the god" / "revealing his face" (mAA nTr / wn-Hr) and the "appearance" (xaw) and "coming out" (prt) of the statue. Both include the statue's purification, anointing, clothing with linen and adornment with regalia. In the daily ritual everything stays inside the sanctuary. In festivals the statues come out in procession, which Coppens frames as the "passive" daily cult versus the "active" festival. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- The procession from the dark sanctuary or crypt out to sunlight and the pylon evokes the sun's journey and rebirth. The pylon symbolises the horizon. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- Eleven temple calendars survive at Edfu, Kom Ombo, Dendera and Esna (Alliot 1949–54: 197–302; Cauville 2002; Grimm 1994; Sauneron 1962). They give mainly the festival's name, date and length, which can run "from a single day to several weeks". They do not allow detailed reconstruction of the rites. The priestly synodal decrees of the Ptolemaic court are a further indirect source. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- "Most of the feasts mentioned in the temple calendars and priestly decrees can be dated to much earlier periods in Egyptian history; nonetheless, for a significant number of festivals we possess few references from prior eras." — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)

**1. New Year / "Opening of the Year" (wp-rnpt)**
- **Status:** a national festival held in all temples, best known from Edfu and Dendera, and held entirely within the temple walls. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Dates:** rites often began as early as the end of Mesore and continued through the five epagomenal days. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Sequence on New Year's Day:**
  - Statues are carried from their chapels and crypts to the wabet and court complex.
  - There they are purified, clothed and adorned.
  - The procession then goes to a kiosk on the temple roof.
  - On the roof, the "opening of the mouth" and exposure to sunlight revitalise the statues and reunite them with their ba.
  - Sources: Alliot 1949–54: 303–433; Fairman 1954–55: 183–189. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Popular account:** Watterson dates it to the first day of the Inundation season. In her account the climax is at midday, when the sun shines on the statues of Horus and Hathor on the roof. She also says the temple consecration, "Handing over the House to its Lord", was repeated every New Year's Day. — [Watterson 2000](https://ancientegyptarchive.co.uk/temples04.htm)

**2. Beautiful Feast of Behdet / "Beautiful Meeting" (sHn nfr n BHdt)**
- **Egyptian name, month and themes:** sHn nfr n BHdt, held in the month of Epiphi. Its central themes are fertility and regeneration. It was "in essence a popular festival" because much of it took place outside the temple precinct. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Primary source:** the walls of the open court at Edfu (Chassinat, Edfou V: 28–35, 124–136; Edfou X, pls. cxxi–cxxii, cxxvi–cxxvii). — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Sequence:**
  - The statue of Hathor travels about 180 km by boat from Dendera to Edfu.
  - It halts at towns including Thebes and Hierakonpolis to visit local deities.
  - From Hierakonpolis onward, the local Horus accompanies Hathor in his own boat.
  - Pilgrims gather along the way, and official delegations (e.g. from Elephantine and Hierakonpolis) come to Edfu.
  - The central act is a visit by the main deities of Edfu (Horus and Hathor) to the necropolis of Behdet to bring offerings to Edfu's ancestor gods.
  - Coppens cites Alliot 1949–54: 442–560; Kurth 1994a ("Die Reise der Hathor von Dendera nach Edfu"); Kurth 1994b: 156–179; Fairman 1954–55: 196–200; Cauville 2002: 59–64; Preys 2002: 556–562. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Purpose:** "the regeneration of the ancestor gods, together with a general regeneration of the whole of Egypt." — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Popular account:** Watterson calls it the "Feast of the Joyous Union". She has it begin at the new moon of the third month of summer and last fifteen days. In her account the divine marriage is consummated on day 1, and the feast then becomes a harvest celebration called the "Festival of Behdet", after which Hathor returns to Dendera "to await the birth of the son". — [Watterson 2000](https://ancientegyptarchive.co.uk/temples04.htm)
- **Alliot on timing (archive.org OCR snippet, garbled):** Alliot says the actual departure day from Dendera shifted in the calendar from year to year ("se déplaçait au calendrier, selon l'année"). He gives more weight to the Edfu day counts than to the Dendera new-moon date. — [Alliot 1954, archive.org](https://ia802901.us.archive.org/31/items/BdE-20.2/BdE%2020-2%20Alliot%2C%20Maurice%20-%20Le%20culte%20d%27Horus%20%C3%A0%20Edfou%20au%20temps%20des%20Ptol%C3%A9m%C3%A9es%20%281954%29%20LR.pdf) (only snippets read)
- **Other secondary claims:** popular sites give a 14-day length and a new-moon arrival. They are not reliable enough to cite as fact. An Egyptian Knowledge Bank (EKB) journal article studies the carrying texts of the Horus and Hathor processions at Edfu. — [EKB article 23510](https://journals.ekb.eg/article_23510.html)

**3. Coronation of the Sacred Falcon (xaw nswt, "appearance of the king")**
- **Date and primary source:** held during the first days of Tybi. It is depicted on the inner face of the enclosure wall (Chassinat, Edfou VI: 92–104, 143–157, 262–274, 298–309; Edfou X: pl. cxlix). — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Theology:** the festival follows the Osiris (Khoiak) rites at the end of Khoiak. On 1 Tybi, the first day of the fifth month, Horus, as son and heir of Osiris, takes the kingship. The festival re-enacts both Horus and the ruling pharaoh taking the throne. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Stages:**
  1. The falcon-headed statue of Horus goes in procession from the sanctuary to the "Temple of the Sacred Falcon" in front of the main temple.
  2. A sacred falcon reared within the precinct is chosen as heir of the god.
  3. The falcon is shown to the crowd from the platform between the pylon wings.
  4. The falcon is crowned in the temple.
  5. A festive meal is held in the Temple of the Sacred Falcon.
  - Sources: Alliot 1949–54: 282–284, 561–676; Drioton 1948; Fairman 1954–55: 189–192. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Van den Hoven (Leiden thesis):**
  - Eight ritual scenes at Edfu depict the ritual. Their order on the walls does not necessarily reflect the order of performance, and the rites were not necessarily performed where they are depicted.
  - The ritual is "a new composition of the Ptolemaic period in which tradition was merged with contemporary ideas". Anointing is a central feature.
  - In Alliot's phasing, the final phase on 1 Tybi was a procession of the falcon and Horus to the Pr-Bik, where the falcon was enthroned and given meat offerings again.
  - Sources: [Leiden handle](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/handle/1887/46027); [search-snippet summaries of the chapters](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2916659/view). I did not read the chapters in full.
- **Popular account:** Watterson gives the first five days of the first month of winter. In her account falconers present birds and one is chosen. She interprets the rite as a symbolic renewal of the king's coronation. — [Watterson 2000](https://ancientegyptarchive.co.uk/temples04.htm)

**4. Festival of Victory (Hb qnt) and the "Triumph of Horus" / "Myth of Horus at Edfu"**
- **Primary source:** depicted on the interior of the enclosure wall (Chassinat, Edfou X: pls. cxlvii–cxlviii; Edfou XIII: pls. ccccxciv–dxiv, dxviii–dxxxiii). It celebrated Horus's victory over his enemies. Coppens cites Alliot 1949–54: 677–822; Blackman & Fairman, JEA 28–30 (1942–44); Fairman, JEA 21 (1935); Fairman 1974, *The Triumph of Horus*. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Date and duration (secondary only):** Watterson says it began on day 21 of the second month of winter and lasted five days. In her account a sacred drama of Horus avenging his father on Seth was performed, with scenes on the west wall of the ambulatory, and each performance ended with a recital of the Legend of the Winged Disk. — [Watterson 2000](https://ancientegyptarchive.co.uk/temples04.htm). The mapping of that date to "21 Mechir" appears only in secondary tables and was not verified against a primary calendar (see Gaps).
- **The drama:**
  - **Actors and roles:** a lector priest; Horus of Behdet; Horus of Mesen; Isis; Thoth (reading from a papyrus); the king (Ptolemy IX) and queen (Cleopatra III); Seth as a hippopotamus "played in the water"; and a chorus.
  - **Setting and reading:** the prologue's action is set on the Sacred Lake. Piccione calls the work a mix of "drama, religious liturgy, and spectacle".
  - **Dating:** Piccione dates a probable first performance to about 110 BC, early in the reign of Ptolemy IX.
  - Source: [Piccione, based on Fairman](https://piccionep.people.charleston.edu/hist270/horuspro.html)
- **The harpooning:** Horus (with the king) drives successive harpoons into Seth as a hippopotamus, and the text counts harpoons (e.g. "fifth harpoon", "seventh harpoon"). — [Piccione scene pages](https://piccionep.people.charleston.edu/hist270/horus1i.html). Drioton published a French reconstruction, "Le texte dramatique d'Edfou" (CASAE 11, 1948). — [archive.org](https://ia804609.us.archive.org/11/items/cASAE-11-1948/cASAE%2011%20Drioton%2C%20Etienne%20-%20Le%20texte%20dramatique%20d%27Edfou%20%281948%29%20LR.pdf)
- **Scholarly debate:** whether this was a staged "play" is a reconstruction. Fairman and Drioton favoured a dramatic reading, and Piccione and the Toledo article follow it. The Toledo article claims annual performance. — [UToledo article](https://openjournals.utoledo.edu/index.php/prs/article/view/590/378)

**5. Foundation and consecration rites (Watterson's list, from the Edfu reliefs)**
- **Rites shown:** Stretching the Cord, Hacking the Ground, Making Bricks, Scattering Sand, Placing the (foundation) Bricks and deposits, Scattering Besen (purification), and Offerings (bull and goose heads). These are followed by "Handing over the House to its Lord". — [Watterson 2000](https://ancientegyptarchive.co.uk/temples04.htm)

**6. Daily ritual (Watterson)**
- **Sequence:**
  - Before dawn: water is drawn from the sacred lake and the food offerings are prepared.
  - At dawn: the officiant opens the shrine, censes the statue and dresses it in white, green, red and blue cloths.
  - Afterwards: the offerings revert to the priests by rank.
  - Source: [Watterson 2000](https://ancientegyptarchive.co.uk/temples04.htm)

### Inferences
- The 1 Tybi coronation pairs the Osiris death and resurrection (Khoiak) with Horus's accession, so the calendar enacts the Osiris–Horus succession myth as a seasonal cycle. This rests on Coppens's statement of the sequence.
- Where the 21-day count is given, the Festival of Victory falls later in winter than the coronation. The calendar thus orders legitimation (coronation) before the defeat of Seth (victory).
- The detailed ritual sequences known for Edfu are Ptolemaic compositions or redactions. Van den Hoven says so explicitly for the coronation, and the drama dates to Ptolemy IX.

### Gaps
- I could not access the exact calendar dates and lengths for the Behdet festival from a primary edition. Popular sources give the new moon of Epiphi and 14 or 15 days, and Alliot argues the date was variable. Kurth 1994a was not accessible.
- The Victory festival date ("21 Mechir", 5 days) was found only in Watterson and secondary tables. It needs checking in Alliot 677–822 or the Edfu calendar.
- I did not find an independent "Festival of Behdet" separate from the sHn nfr. Watterson treats the "Festival of Behdet" as the later harvest phase of the same feast.
- I did not reach the full text of van den Hoven's ritual reconstruction (falcon selection and anointing details, the exact locations).
- The Edfou project (Kurth, Edfou-Projekt, Hamburg/Göttingen) publications were not reached online.

## Other Horus cult centres (Hierakonpolis, Kom Ombo, Buto, Letopolis, Qus, Mesen, Nubian Horus forms; Dendera; Harsomtus)

### Takeaway
Outside Edfu, the web sources mostly attest that local Horus cults and temples existed. They rarely describe specific festivals. Nekhen has the earliest archaeological cult evidence (Predynastic to Early Dynastic). The Nubian Horus forms are documented from the Middle Kingdom and New Kingdom. Kom Ombo has festival calendars, but their content was not accessed. Hierakonpolis and Horus of Nekhen take part in the Edfu Behdet festival.

### Cited Findings
- **Hierakonpolis/Nekhen in the Behdet festival:** Hathor's flotilla stops at Hierakonpolis, the local Horus joins her from there in his own boat, and Hierakonpolis sends a delegation to Edfu. This is a Ptolemaic text from the Edfu court. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Nekhen archaeology:**
  - The early temple is an oval courtyard about 32 x 13 m with a reed fence and probably a tall pole topped by a falcon emblem.
  - A golden falcon-headed statue of Horus was found buried in the floor of the later temple.
  - The Narmer Palette and other objects were deposited under the temple floor (the "Main Deposit").
  - The idea that the temple was decommissioned after Narmer's coronation or Heb-sed is a hypothesis (Adams), not established fact.
  - The temple stayed in use into the Ptolemaic period.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia: Nekhen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierakonpolis); [Ancient Egypt Online](https://ancientegyptonline.co.uk/temples-nekhen/); [Archaeology magazine](https://archive.archaeology.org/0311/abstracts/hierakonpolis.html). These are tertiary or popular sources, to be checked against Friedman or Adams.
- **Kom Ombo:** the temple serves two triads: Haroeris (Horus the Elder), Tasenetnofret ("the good sister") and Panebtawy ("Lord of the Two Lands"); and Sobek, Hathor and Khonsu. It contains several festival calendars. — [Global Egyptian Museum](https://www.globalegyptianmuseum.org/glossary.aspx?id=214); [Egymonuments](https://egymonuments.gov.eg/monuments/kom-ombo-temple/); calendars also confirmed by [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- **Horus of Buhen:**
  - "Horus, Lord of Buhen" was the main deity at Buhen from the Middle Kingdom (Senusret).
  - Ahmose set up a sanctuary in the north of the fortress.
  - Hatshepsut built a temple over the Middle Kingdom one, and Thutmose III extended it.
  - Sources: [Heidelberg propylaeum](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/propylaeumdok/id/eprint/6206/contents); [Randall-MacIver & Woolley, Buhen](https://archive.org/download/buhentext00rand/buhentext00rand.pdf)
- **Horus of Miam (Aniba):**
  - Priestly titles show a temple of Horus of Miam from early Dynasty 18 to late Dynasty 20.
  - Rock-cut statues of Horus of Miam, Satet and Thutmose III stand at Ellesiya.
  - Sources: [Heidelberg propylaeum](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/propylaeumdok/id/eprint/6220/contents); [Museo Egizio, Ellesiya](https://archiviofotografico.museoegizio.it/en/archive/nubia/rock-cut-chapel-of-ellesiya/?ff=1)
- **Wadi es-Sebua (Ramesses II):** four falcon-headed sphinxes represent Horus of Miam, Horus of Meha, Horus of Baki (Quban) and Horus of Edfu. The smaller temple may originally have been dedicated to a local Nubian Horus. — [ancient-history-sites](https://www.ancient-history-sites.com/?p=133319) (popular source)
- **Dendera:**
  - Many statues travelled to the bark station and sacred lake in Tybi.
  - Hathor processed to the mammisi six times a year.
  - The "Festival of Intoxication" (Hb tx) in Thoth marked the return and enthronement of the "raging goddess from the South".
  - Sources: Cauville 2002; Sternberg-el-Hotabi 1992. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)

### Inferences
- The Edfu Behdet festival also shows a regional network of Horus cults: Hierakonpolis takes part and Elephantine sends a delegation. Horus of Nekhen therefore had a ritual role in the Ptolemaic period, not only an archaeological one.
- The Nubian Horus forms (Buhen, Miam, Baki, Meha) appear tied to Egyptian royal and military presence. Their evidence is architectural and titular, not festival texts.

### Gaps
- I found no online source with specific festival or ritual data for Buto (Pe), Letopolis (Horus Khenty-irty / Khenty-khem), Qus (Haroeris and Hathor), Horus of Mesen, or Harsomtus/Horus-sema-tawy (at Dendera and Khadi). Harsomtus is the divine child of the Horus and Hathor union at Edfu and Dendera, but I found no web citation on his festival role, so this is training knowledge only.
- The content of the Kom Ombo calendars (Grimm 1994) was not accessed.
- Pyramid Texts and Old Kingdom evidence for "Souls of Pe and Nekhen" rituals were not researched.

## Animal cult: falcon necropolises and votive falcon mummies

### Takeaway
Mass votive falcon mummies at North Saqqara and other sites are mainly Late and Ptolemaic phenomena, starting around Dynasty 26. Falcons were bred for the purpose and offered by pilgrims, probably at festivals. This is distinct from Edfu's single living "sacred falcon", which was chosen and crowned yearly.

### Cited Findings
- Falcons were bred at Saqqara and Abusir. Pilgrims could buy falcon mummies in metal coffins as offerings to Horus cults. Votive mummies became widespread from Dynasty 26 (about 664 BC). — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/magazine/article/animal-mummies)
- Animal mummies fall into four types: pets, victual, cult animals and votive offerings. Votive mummies are the most common, and the most numerous were offered at yearly festivals of animal cults. — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/magazine/2009/11/animal-mummies/); see also [Antiquity, "Imaging the gods", Tomb 3508 North Saqqara](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/D01E2D492C05E8EFEFBBE7EDC4C04584)
- **North Saqqara:**
  - Emery's excavations (1964–71, Egypt Exploration Society) found the Mother of Apis catacomb and catacombs of ibises, falcons and baboons.
  - Davies & Smith 2005 is the falcon complex report.
  - Falcon mummies are often incomplete, and some are built from ibis remains wrapped in falcon form.
  - Mother of Apis inscriptions date to 534–41 BC.
  - J.D. Ray's *The Archive of Hor* (1976) documents the bird cults.
  - Sources: [World Archaeology](https://www.world-archaeology.com/features/north-saqqara/); [MDPI Heritage 2022](https://mdpi.com/2571-9408/5/3/77); [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL38253344M)
- Images of child Horus are found mixed with falcon-shaped coffins in falcon cemeteries, as if they served similar votive functions. — [Brooklyn Museum](https://brooklynmuseum.org/opencollection/objects/117069) / [Emory Carlos Museum](https://carlos.emory.edu/publications/LifeAndTheAfterlife/catalog/sacred-life/54)

### Inferences
- Votive falcon mummies reflect personal piety, a pilgrim's offering to Horus as intermediary, and festival-linked deposition. Edfu's crowned falcon reflects the royal and theological "living image" (ba) of Horus. These are two different ritual logics.

### Gaps
- I found no detailed source on the Abydos falcon burials. Oracle practices at the Saqqara falcon cult (Hor archive) were not accessed in detail.
- I did not determine whether Edfu had a falcon necropolis linked to the crowned falcons.

## Ptolemaic–Roman vs earlier attestation

### Takeaway
Almost all detailed ritual scripts (sequences, actors, dialogue) come from Ptolemaic temple walls, chiefly Edfu. Earlier periods attest the existence of Horus cults (Nekhen from Predynastic times, Buhen from the Middle Kingdom, Miam in the New Kingdom). They give little procedural detail.

### Cited Findings
- Most calendar feasts date back to earlier periods, but many have few earlier references. — [Coppens 2009](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4cd7q9mn)
- The falcon coronation is "a new composition of the Ptolemaic period". — [van den Hoven 2017](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/handle/1887/46027)
- The Edfu drama is dated to about 110 BC (Ptolemy IX) and "draws on much older archetypes". — [Piccione](https://piccionep.people.charleston.edu/hist270/horuspro.html)

### Inferences
- **Verified Ptolemaic:** the Edfu Behdet festival texts, the coronation, the Victory drama and the New Year procession. **Earlier, archaeological only:** the Nekhen cult and the Nubian Horus temples. **Late Period onward:** mass votive falcon mummies.
- **Unverifiable / modern esoteric:** online claims calling the Edfu drama "the earliest play ever written" (Watterson's popular phrasing) or presenting exact Gregorian months as fact. Esoteric "Horus initiation" claims were not encountered and should be treated as having no ancient basis unless sourced.

### Gaps
- I found no web-accessible pre-Ptolemaic evidence for a Hathor journey to Edfu or a falcon coronation. Scholarly literature (e.g. Kurth, Alliot) may discuss earlier antecedents, but this could not be verified.
