## 1. Coronation, the Horus name (serekh), sema-tawy, and the "baptism of Pharaoh"

### Takeaway
The coronation was a drawn-out process lasting up to a year, not a single day. Its rites included the "Uniting of the Two Lands", the "Circuit of the (White) Walls" and the "Appearance of the King". The Horus name, written in the serekh, is the oldest royal title, so the new king took office *as* Horus. The coronation imagery includes Horus and Seth binding the heraldic plants (sema-tawy) and Horus and Thoth purifying the king (the "baptism of Pharaoh"). That imagery is attested from the Middle Kingdom into the Roman period.

### Cited Findings
- **Horus name:** It is "the oldest attested name/title of Egyptian kings" — [UCL Digital Egypt, Horus name](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt//ideology/kingname/horus.html). The five-part titulary taken at accession (Horus, Two Ladies, Golden Horus, throne name, birth name) is treated in R. Leprohon, "Patterns of Royal Name-giving", *UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology* (2010) — [escholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/51b2647c.pdf). Only the metadata was confirmed, not the article's content.
- **Coronation as a year-long process:** "Not a single event but rather a long lasting process including several festivals, rites and ceremonies lasting up to a full year". Egyptologists speak of the "year of the coronation", and that first year was not counted among the regnal years — [Wikipedia, Coronation of the pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_feast_(Egypt)). This is a secondary source drawing on Wilkinson, *Early Dynastic Egypt* (2001); Schott, *Altägyptische Festdaten* (1950); and Barta, *SAK* 8 (1980).
- **Rites listed in the same source:**
  - Unification of Upper and Lower Egypt, thought to derive from the predynastic "smiting the enemy" rite.
  - Circumambulation of the White Walls of Memphis.
  - The Appearance of the King, in three steps: *khaj-nisut*, *khaj-bitj* and *khaj-nisut-bitj*. Its introduction is credited to Khasekhemwy, and the earliest mention is under Djoser. It was repeated every second year.
  - The Sokar feast.
  - The Sed festival, first celebrated in the coronation year.

  Source: [Wikipedia, Coronation of the pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_feast_(Egypt)).
- **Sema-tawy:** On a throne relief of Senusret I, "Horus and Seth wrap papyrus and lotus haulms around a trachea" (the *sma* sign). This represents the lasting unification of the Two Lands — [Wikipedia, Coronation of the pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_feast_(Egypt)).
- **Horus and Seth in the serekh:** Khasekhemwy's serekh is topped by both Horus and Seth, which is "likely to commemorate his achievement of reunifying Egypt" — [Wikipedia, Palermo Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palermo_Stone).
- **"Baptism of Pharaoh" (Roman period):** Met cornice block 11.154.3 is sandstone, from the Temple of Harendotes ("Horus the avenger of his father") at Philae, dated 41–68 CE. It shows a purification rite that was "part of Egyptian coronation ceremonies". Horus (now lost) and the ibis-headed Thoth pour streams of *ankh* and *was* signs over the king, probably Claudius or Nero — [Metropolitan Museum 547283](https://metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/547283).
- **Baptism variant (New Kingdom):** Naville, *Deir el-Bahari* (1896), describes a "baptism" of Hatshepsut performed "not however by Horus and Set, but by Horus and Amon" — [Naville 1896, Heidelberg digital](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/naville1896bd2/0023). The god pairs vary: Horus and Thoth, Horus and Seth, Horus and Amun.
- **Late cultic form, the Edfu falcon:** At Edfu, a living sacred falcon was enthroned as king in a procession and received kingship from Horus Behdety. This is a temple ritual that reuses the royal coronation pattern — [Leiden dissertation, *The coronation ritual of the falcon at Edfu: tradition and innovation in ancient Egyptian ritual composition*](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2916662/view). The author's name was not captured in this session.
- **Britannica on timing:** The coronation was seen as a "cosmic new beginning" and generally coincided with a new beginning in nature, such as the New Year — [Britannica, sacred kingship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sacred-kingship/Forms-and-types-of-sacred-legitimation-of-kings).

### Inferences
- The Horus title, the sema-tawy and the baptism scenes all cast the living king as Horus, the legitimate heir who unites, or reconciles, the domains of Horus and Seth. Divine purification by Horus prepares him for that role.
- The god pairs in the baptism scenes vary (Horus+Thoth, Horus+Seth, Horus+Amun). That suggests the scene is a flexible iconographic formula, not a fixed liturgy. This is an inference.

### Gaps
- **Gardiner's article:** It is generally cited as A.H. Gardiner, "The Baptism of Pharaoh", *JEA* 36 (1950), 3–12. The search did not reach it, so the reference and pages need checking. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
- **Frankfort on Horus and Osiris:** Frankfort, *Kingship and the Gods* (1948), describes the living king as Horus and the dead king as Osiris. This could not be checked or quoted. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
- **Kuhlmann, "Throne":** The UEE entry (2011) is at [escholarship 8xc7k559](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8xc7k559/qt8xc7k559.pdf). Only its title page was retrieved.
- **Other coronation texts:** The Horemheb coronation inscription (Turin statue) and Hatshepsut's coronation texts were not retrieved.

## 2. Sed festival (heb-sed): Horus aspects, Narmer macehead, Djoser reliefs

### Takeaway
The Sed festival renewed the king's rule. Its signature rite was the ritual run between boundary markers. That run is attested on a label of Den (1st Dynasty) and in Djoser's Step Pyramid reliefs and Heb-Sed court (3rd Dynasty). Sources on Horus-specific aspects were not reached.

### Cited Findings
- **Den:** An ebony label shows Den running around ritual boundary markers. Cited to Wilkinson, *Early Dynastic Egypt* — [Wikipedia, Sed festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sed_festival).
- **Djoser:** Reliefs in the Step Pyramid's underground galleries show him running the Heb-Sed. The complex includes a Heb-Sed court with two boundary stones — [Wikipedia, Sed festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sed_festival).
- **Later examples:** Pepi I (alabaster jars for his first Sed festival), Sahure, Amenhotep III, Akhenaten and Osorkon II at Bubastis — [Wikipedia, Sed festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sed_festival). The standard study is Hornung & Staehelin, *Neue Studien zum Sedfest* (2006) (same source, further reading).
- **Timing:** The Sed festival was first celebrated in the coronation year, then in year 30, then about every third year — [Wikipedia, Coronation of the pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_feast_(Egypt)). The idea of a first Sed in the coronation year is a debated reconstruction.
- **Regicide theory:** The festival "might have been instituted to replace a ritual of executing a pharaoh" — [Wikipedia, Sed festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sed_festival). This is a speculative older theory. It is a scholarly reconstruction with no direct evidence and should not be presented as fact.
- **Further literature:** A *JNES* article, "The Egyptian Sed-Festival Rites", exists — [U. Chicago Journals](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/371833). It is paywalled and was not read.

### Inferences
- The king entering the festival as Horus (Horus name on the Sed reliefs) and the falcon-standard processions are commonly cited Horus elements. They were not confirmed in this session.

### Gaps
- **Narmer macehead:** The usual reading is that it shows a king enthroned in a pavilion with a Sed-like scene, though whether it is a Sed or a victory ceremony is debated. No source was retrieved. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
- **Horus-specific rites:** No source was read on Horus-specific rites within the Sed, such as the "Souls of Pe and Nekhen" or Horus standards. Frankfort's chapter on the Sed festival was not accessible.

## 3. "Following of Horus" (Shemsu-Hor): royal progress vs. mythical ancestors

### Takeaway
The phrase has two quite different meanings:
1. **A historical administrative and ritual event.** The early royal annals record the *šms-Ḥr* ("following/worship of Horus") roughly every two years in the 1st–2nd Dynasties. It is interpreted as a royal tour of the country tied to assessing wealth (the cattle count).
2. **A mythical group.** The "Followers of Horus" were semi-divine predecessor kings who ruled before Menes in later king-lists and texts.

### Cited Findings
- **Sethe's reading:** An early 20th-century report, citing Sethe, says the term means those devoted to Horus, his worshippers, not his successors or companions. It reports that the Palermo Stone mentions a royal "worship of Horus" every second year — [Archaeological Report 1902–03, Heidelberg](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/archaeological_report1902_1903/0036). This is an old source, and its Hierakonpolis link needs checking.
- **Tour and cattle count:** The Shemsu-Hor is described as a royal tour to assess and tax crops and livestock, later linked to the cattle count. The Palermo Stone ties the count explicitly to the event only from the 2nd Dynasty. Sources disagree on whether it was annual or biennial. Pepi I is the first king with certainly attested yearly counts — [Wikipedia, Cattle count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle_count); [Cambridge Core reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/0DB61A5A16C5DC5ABEEDEAEADEE52533/core-reader); popular summary at [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1012/ancient-egyptian-taxes--the-cattle-count/). WHE is a popular source, so use it with caution.
- **Palermo Stone:** It is one of the fragments of the Old Kingdom Royal Annals. Schäfer published it in 1902. The standard study is Wilkinson, *Royal Annals of Ancient Egypt* (2000) — [Wikipedia, Palermo Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palermo_Stone); [Hsu 2010 PDF](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com:443/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/Egypt-Libya/PalermoStone-Hsu2010.pdf).
- **Modern use for Dynasty 0 rulers:** "Followers of Horus" is also used for predynastic rulers whose names contain "Hor", such as Iry-Hor and Ny-Hor. Kahl cites a volume titled *The Followers of Horus* (Dreyer's chapter) — [Kahl 2006, Heidelberg](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/propylaeumdok/3706/1/Kahl_Inscriptional_evidence_2006.pdf).

### Inferences
- The annalistic Shemsu-Hor is a real, recurring royal ritual and administrative event. The mythical "Followers of Horus" belong to later theology and historiography. The two should not be conflated.
- Fringe literature turns the Shemsu-Hor into a lost pre-Egyptian civilisation. This is unverifiable and esoteric, and no academic source supports it.

### Gaps
- **Mythical Followers in king-lists:** Their occurrences in the Turin Canon and Manetho's "spirits of the dead" were not retrieved. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
- **Wilkinson's own wording:** His text on the Shemsu-Hor was not accessed.

## 4. Ramesseum Dramatic Papyrus (P. BM EA 10610)

### Takeaway
It is an illustrated ritual roll of the 12th Dynasty (c. 1980 BCE) in which the king, in the role of Horus, appears repeatedly. Its purpose is disputed. Sethe (1928) read it as a coronation drama, others as a Sed festival. Recent work (Geisen 2018) reads it as a commemoration or funerary ritual for the deceased Senusret I.

### Cited Findings
- **Description:** A ceremonial play "celebrating the coronation or the Sed festival performed for Senusret I". The king "in the role of Horus, appears multiple times". The vignettes run in a comic-strip sequence beneath vertical columns of text. It is the oldest known illustrated papyrus roll. It was found in 1896 by Quibell and Petrie in a shaft tomb under the Ramesseum and is now in the British Museum — [Wikipedia, Dramatic Ramesseum Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_Ramesseum_Papyrus).
- **Sethe:** The 1928 edition was the basis of decades of debate — same source.
- **Geisen:** Christina Geisen, *A Commemoration Ritual for Senwosret I: P. BM EA 10610.1-5 / P. Ramesseum B* (2018), argues that it is a ritual commemorating the deceased king — [Zenon DAI record](https://zenon.dainst.org/Record/001556305/TOC); [Schweitzer listing](https://www.schweitzer-online.de/en/buch/Geisen/Commemoration-Ritual-for-Senwosret-I/9780974002590/A43345934/).
- **Gestermann and Quack:** L. Gestermann, "Das Ritual des dramatischen Ramesseumpapyrus" (2008), also discusses its interpretation — [Heidelberg Propylaeum PDF](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/propylaeumdok/3053/1/Gestermann_Das_Ritual_des_dramatischen_2008.pdf). Access was blocked, so the content was not read. A 2006 article by J.F. Quack on reading and interpreting the papyrus is cited there, but its argument could not be retrieved.

### Inferences
- The debate matters for Horus theology. On a coronation reading, the living king *is* Horus. On a funerary or commemorative reading, Horus is the role of the officiating successor acting for his dead father as Osiris. That is the same Horus–Osiris pattern as the Opening of the Mouth.

### Gaps
- **Scene content:** No verified details were obtained, such as the number of scenes or episodes like grain threshed by cattle as Osiris beaten by Seth, or the Djed pillar raising. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
- **Helck's and Altenmüller's positions** were not retrieved.

## 5. Mortuary-royal rites: Opening of the Mouth, the Eye of Horus offering, and the daily ritual

### Takeaway
In the Opening of the Mouth, the officiant (the sem priest or the son) plays Horus and the deceased is Osiris. The adze rite echoes Horus opening his father's mouth. In offering liturgies, offerings are named "the Eye of Horus" (for example wine as the "Green Eye of Horus"). The principal sources for the daily temple ritual are the Berlin papyri (P. Berlin 3055 ff., the "ritual of Amenhotep I/Amun") and the Seti I reliefs at Abydos.

### Cited Findings
- **Horus and Osiris roles:** The deceased is regarded as Osiris and the sem priest represents Horus, safeguarding the body as Horus protected his father. The rite could be performed by the son in a leopard skin. The sem priest declares that he has seen his father in all his forms. Otto published the text from Padiamenope (TT 33) in 1960 (*Das ägyptische Mundöffnungsritual*) — [Wikipedia, Opening of the mouth ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opening_of_the_mouth_ceremony). The search summary flags that page as partly garbled.
- **Pyramid Texts:** The Pyramid Text formula as summarized there: "Horus has split open the King's mouth, using that with which he split open his father's mouth". The restored Eye of Horus symbolizes renewal — [Wikipedia, Opening of the mouth ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opening_of_the_mouth_ceremony). The exact PT spell numbers were not verified.
- **Eye of Horus thesis:** S. Edwards wrote a Swansea PhD thesis on the Eye of Horus in the Pyramid Texts, with a chapter on its role in the offering ritual — [Swansea cronfa PDF](https://cronfa.swansea.ac.uk/Record/cronfa16459/Download/0016459-12062014101423.pdf). Only the table of contents was seen.
- **Wine:** Offering liturgies call wine metaphorically the "Green Eye of Horus", linked to rejuvenation (Mu-chou Poo, on liquids in temple ritual) — cited via search result; see [UEE escholarship galley](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/nelc_uee/article/3991/galley/2567/download). The exact attribution of this file needs confirmation.
- **Daily ritual sources:** The two principal sources are the inscribed scenes in Seti I's temple at Abydos and the Berlin papyri. P. Berlin 3055 holds the daily ritual of Amun, with a later version from Tebtunis — [UCL Digital Egypt, daily cult](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/religion/dailycult.html). The search snippet was used; the page returned 403 on fetch.
- **Nelson 1949:** H.H. Nelson, "Certain reliefs at Karnak and Medinet Habu and the ritual of Amenophis I", *JNES* 8 (1949), is a key study of the Amenhotep I ritual (cited in a Leiden publication; content not read) — [Leiden](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2916658/view).

### Inferences
- In this ritual logic, the king (or the priest acting for him) offers as Horus. The offering is the restored Eye, given back to the god or to Osiris. This is consistent with the sources above, but the theological generalization should be attributed to secondary literature such as Assmann and Otto.

### Gaps
- **Offering formula:** The claim that the Eye of Horus is the paradigm of *all* offerings, and that the *ḥtp-dj-nswt* offering formula is spoken by the king as Horus, is a widely cited scholarly view (e.g. Assmann, *Death and Salvation*). No quotation or page was retrieved. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
- **Berlin papyri details:** The number of the Amenhotep I papyrus (P. Berlin 3014 + 3053 are usually cited) and Moret's 1902 edition (*Le rituel du culte divin journalier*) were not verified. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**

## 6. Victory and execration rituals: smiting, driving the calves, striking the ball, Horus vs. Seth

### Takeaway
"Smiting the enemy" is the oldest royal victory image (Narmer Palette). It is thought to underlie the "Uniting the Two Lands" rite. "Driving the calves" and "Striking the ball" are New Kingdom to Ptolemaic temple rites. The sources found tie them to Amun and to Hathor/Apophis, not explicitly to the king acting as Horus.

### Cited Findings
- **Smiting and the Narmer Palette:** The unification rite "likely derived from the predynastic 'smiting of the enemy' ritual". The Narmer Palette shows smiting on one side and entwined serpopards on the other — [Wikipedia, Coronation of the pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_feast_(Egypt)).
- **Driving the calves:** Pharaoh drives four calves (red, white, black, spotted) before the god. The example given is Ramesses II before Amun-Re Kamutef — [Karnak Hypostyle Hall Project, ritual scene varieties](https://classic.memphis.edu/hypostyle/reliefs_inscriptions/varieties-ritual-scenes.php); [structure of ritual scenes](https://classic.memphis.edu/hypostyle/reliefs_inscriptions/structure-ritual-scenes.php).
- **Striking the ball (Met):** A Met relief (380–246 BCE) shows the king striking a ball before a goddess, possibly Hathor. The ball represents the evil eye of Apophis — [Met 545991](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545991).
- **Striking the ball (Kent thesis):** A Kent thesis describes the rite as smashing clay balls to destroy the eye of Apophis — [Kent KAR](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/686788).

### Inferences
- The king's role as Horus in these rites cannot be confirmed from what was retrieved. The sources found cast him as the god's servant, or as the destroyer of Apophis on the sun-god's behalf.
- Edfu Ptolemaic material (the Triumph of Horus/Myth of Horus drama, the harpooning of the Seth hippopotamus) is the clearest case of Horus–Seth combat as state ritual. It was not retrieved in this session.

### Gaps
- **Triumph of Horus:** No source was read on the Edfu "Triumph of Horus" (Blackman & Fairman). **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
- **Execration rituals:** No source was read on execration rituals, such as the Mirgissa deposit or breaking red pots, or on any link between them and Horus.

## 7. Royal birth (divine birth cycle) and Horus

### Takeaway
The divine birth cycle shows the king as the son of Amun begotten on the queen. Its earliest pictorial version is Hatshepsut's at Deir el-Bahari, and Amenhotep III copied it at Luxor (Brunner 1964/1986). Horus's specific role in the cycle could not be confirmed. Naville records a related "baptism" of Hatshepsut by Horus and Amun.

### Cited Findings
- **Hatshepsut:** Her cycle is the earliest known pictorial version. Amun convenes the gods to announce the coming queen. The scenes were later vandalized — [Wikipedia, Depiction of Hatshepsut's birth and coronation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depiction_of_Hatshepsut%27s_birth_and_coronation).
- **Amenhotep III:** Naville writes that at Luxor "several of the texts are identical, word for word", and that Amenhotep III "merely copied" Deir el-Bahari — [Naville 1896](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/naville1896bd2/0017).
- **Brunner:** H. Brunner, *Die Geburt des Gottkönigs* (1964; 2nd ed. 1986), is the standard study. A review argues that his thesis of an Old Kingdom origin is "no longer tenable" — [Scripta (journal.fi)](https://journal.fi/scripta/article/view/67291/27589).
- **Baptism of Hatshepsut:** Naville describes it as performed by Horus and Amun — [Naville 1896](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/naville1896bd2/0023).

### Inferences
- The birth cycle grounds kingship in Amun's paternity rather than in Horus. Horus enters through the coronation and purification sequence that follows the birth, and through the Horus name given to the child. This is an inference.

### Gaps
- **Khnum and Heqet:** Khnum fashions the child and its ka on the potter's wheel and Heqet gives life. These roles are standard but no source was retrieved. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
- **Mammisi:** The Ptolemaic and Roman "birth houses" (Dendera, Edfu, Philae) transfer the birth myth to the child god (Harsomtus/Horus-sa-Iset). No source was retrieved. **[UNVERIFIED – background]**
