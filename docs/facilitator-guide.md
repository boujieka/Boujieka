# Facilitator guide

How to run these films in a room of ministers, cabinet officials, regulators,
utility and treasury staff, developers or development partners.

## What the films are for

The chapter's argument is not that MoUs are bad. It is that an MoU is signed
**before either of two tests has been applied**, and that the people who will pay
for it are not in the room when it is signed. The films are designed to make an
audience feel the second half of that sentence, because the first half is already
widely accepted and changes nothing on its own.

Everyone in the sector can describe the MoU trap. It persists anyway. That is the
problem the training has to address — so the films spend their weight on incentives
and on who is absent, not on explaining what an MoU is.

## Choosing what to show

| Time available | Show |
| --- | --- |
| 10 minutes | The relevant audience module alone |
| 20 minutes | Audience module, then discussion on its ask card |
| 45 minutes | Core film, then the audience module, then discussion |
| Mixed-audience workshop | Core film together, then split into groups by module |

The modules are self-contained. They repeat what they need from the core film, so
they work without it.

## Discussion prompts that work

After the **who gains, who pays** scene:

- In our last three power MoUs, was the utility a party? Was the ministry of
  finance shown the text before signature?
- Who in this room has authority to say no to a signature, and at what point?

After **two exits**:

- Name an MoU we hold today that we would not sign again. What is stopping it from
  lapsing?
- What would it cost us to publish our register tomorrow?

After the **ask card**:

- Which of these four can we do without new legislation?
- Who owns each one by name?

## Handling the obvious objections

**"This is about badly run countries, not us."**
Qua Iboe had an international oil major, a private-equity-owned infrastructure
company, one of Nigeria's largest industrial groups and the national oil company as
sponsors. It stalled anyway. The chapter is explicit that capable ministries sign
weak MoUs too, because the incentive does not depend on capacity: a signature is
rewarded today and paid for later, by someone else.

**"You are telling us not to sign MoUs."**
No. The Nachtigal scene exists precisely to make that clear. Some projects cannot be
tendered at the outset because nobody yet knows enough to write a tender. The claim
is narrower: an MoU checked against the plan, carrying milestones and a sunset date,
reviewed by the utility and the ministry of finance, and published, is not a trap.

**"Development securities would have prevented this."**
Nigeria is the counter-example, and the films say so. Securities were reportedly
posted and the projects still stalled — over the tariff, the buyer and state support.
Pricing the option is one of four remedies, not a solution on its own.

**"Where does that number come from?"**
Every screen carrying a figure carries its source, and flags press-sourced numbers
as press-sourced. `docs/evidence.md` has the full list with the chapter's own
qualifications. Do not defend a number further than the chapter does.

## A note on what these films do not claim

They do not give a success rate for MoUs. Nobody knows it — the denominator has
never been counted, and the films say so on screen. If someone in the room asks
"what percentage of MoUs fail?", the honest answer is that the question cannot be
answered from published data, and that this gap is itself part of the problem.
