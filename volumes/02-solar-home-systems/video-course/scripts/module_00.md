# Module 0. Welcome: how the course, the book, the model and the tools fit together

Duration: about 8 minutes. Book: Preface, How the book works with the model, Conventions, Status of this edition, and Chapter 1 section 1.7. Model sheets: Dashboard, Checks, Investment_Readiness. Templates and tools: T01 to T08, D1 to D6.

## Learning objectives
1. State the central question of the course and the three businesses inside a PAYGo company.
2. Describe how the book, MODEL 2 (the PAYGo Company Financial and Investment Model), the SolaraPay case, the templates and the decision tools fit together.
3. Apply the course conventions and honesty rules when quoting any figure from the course.

## Scene 1. One question
On screen: Title card. "Can PAYGo solar become profitable and financeable?"

This course is built around one question. Can pay as you go solar, which we will call PAYGo from now on, become profitable and financeable?

The record of the sector so far suggests that the answer can be yes. But only for companies that understand what kind of business they are running. That understanding is the subject of everything that follows.

The course accompanies Book 2 of the Africa Energy Finance collection, "PAYGo Solar Finance: Business Models, Credit Risk and Financial Structuring for Solar Home Systems", by Emmanuel Boujieka Kamga. Each module follows one chapter of the book. Each one supports one decision.

## Scene 2. Three businesses in one
On screen: Three columns. Retailer. Utility like service provider. Consumer lender.

A PAYGo solar home system company, or SHS company, is three businesses at the same time.

It is a retailer, which books margin on the day of sale. It is a utility like service provider, which keeps the device working and the customer engaged for years. And it is a consumer lender. It waits for the cash, a few shillings or naira or kwacha a day, from households whose incomes rise and fall with harvests and remittances.

Many of the strategic and financial mistakes seen in this sector come from analysing one of those businesses and forgetting the other two. A company can look profitable on its income statement and still run out of cash. That is why this course reads PAYGo companies through customer cohorts and the receivables book, not through a simple sales line.

## Scene 3. Who the course is for
On screen: Founders and CFOs. Credit officers. Impact investors. RBF programme managers. Graduate students.

The course is written for the people who have to make decisions about these companies.

Founders and finance directors use it to build a plan that a lender will accept. Credit officers at banks and development finance institutions use it to size a facility. Investment managers at impact funds use it to prepare an investment committee memo. Programme managers use it to design results based financing, which we will call RBF.

It is also meant for graduate students who want to understand how distributed energy access is financed, beyond the headline numbers.

## Scene 4. The map of the course
On screen: Course map. Module 1 foundations. Modules 2 to 5 commercial. Modules 6 to 9 credit and profitability. Modules 10 to 13 cash and capital. Modules 14 to 16 risk, diligence and case. Module 17 model walk through.

The book has sixteen chapters, and the course has a module for each.

Module 1 sets out the business model, and explains why cohorts and the receivables book are the unit of analysis.

Modules 2 to 5 cover the commercial foundations. These are customers and affordability, regulation, product and price plan design, and distribution.

Modules 6 to 9 deal with credit and profitability. You will study repayment behaviour and the key performance indicators, or KPIs, of a portfolio. You will then study revenue recognition, credit losses and unit economics.

Modules 10 to 13 turn to cash and capital. They cover working capital and currency, the funding stack, securitisation and subsidies.

Modules 14 and 15 bring the analysis together for the people who carry the risk, through stress testing and the investor's diligence and memo. Module 16 walks through a complete case. A final module then walks you through the workbook itself, sheet by sheet.

## Scene 5. The companion workbook
On screen: MODEL 2, PAYGo Company Financial and Investment Model, v0.8 (development build). 47 sheets. About 100,900 formulas. 38 integrity tests. Calculation chain from Inputs to Investment readiness.

The book has a companion workbook, MODEL 2, the PAYGo Company Financial and Investment Model. This course uses version 0.8, a development build.

It is a monthly, five year, integrated three statement model. It has 47 sheets and about 100,900 formulas, with no macros and no circular references. Its Checks sheet runs 38 integrity tests. Its outputs have been checked three ways: against a secondary calculation written separately, which agrees to rounding precision, by an independent formula engine, and by a full recalculation in LibreOffice. A test in Microsoft Excel is still pending.

The calculation chain runs from Inputs and Products, through Sales, Customers, PAYGo receivables, the Credit engine, the Vintage engine, and Collections and recoveries. It continues to the RBF engine, Working capital, Financing and the Financial statements. It ends with Covenants, Valuation, Investor returns, the Market benchmark, Calibration and Investment readiness.

The model carries five product tiers. They run from a pico solar kit on a twelve month plan to a large solar inverter with lithium storage on a forty eight month plan.

The chapters refer to the workbook by sheet name, so that each concept can be tested on numbers. You can follow the argument without opening it. If you do work through the sheets, you will finish the course able to build, challenge and defend a PAYGo investment case.

## Scene 6. The SolaraPay case
On screen: SolaraPay Ltd, Republic of Kivara. Fictional company. Synthetic 24 month history. Ask: 4 million dollars equity, 4.5 billion shilling facility.

A worked case runs through every module. SolaraPay Ltd is a fictional company in the fictional Republic of Kivara, with a synthetic twenty four month history.

SolaraPay is asking an impact fund for four million dollars of equity at a pre money valuation of eight million dollars. It is also asking a local bank for a receivables facility of four point five billion shillings.

Its management plan looks attractive. Once the model is calibrated to its own history, the case becomes conditional. The way it moves from one to the other is the method this course teaches.

The workbook never rates a company. Its Investment_Readiness sheet runs 23 gates, counts each one only on evidence, and applies a fixed rule. It reads STOP when a test fails or critical evidence is missing, CONDITIONAL GO when every critical gate is met, and GO only when all 23 are met. Even a GO says only that the evidence file is complete. It is not a recommendation. In its default state the workbook passes 2 of the 23 gates and reads STOP on a failed test: the annual debt service coverage ratio sits below its minimum in Years 1 to 4, so the covenant gate fails. SolaraPay, with its history loaded, passes 4 and reads STOP for the same reason. The analyst in the case still recommends a Conditional Go, and Module 16 explains why the two do not contradict each other.

## Scene 7. Templates and decision tools
On screen: Templates T01 to T08. Decision tools D1 to D6. Book, tools, model: three levels of depth.

Two kits sit beside the book and the model.

The templates pack holds eight working documents, numbered T01 to T08. They are the documents a deal team exchanges during a transaction. They include the business model canvas, the loan tape and data request, the due diligence checklist, the lender KPI report, the borrowing base certificate, the customer credit policy, the agent economics sheet and the investment committee memo.

The decision tools are six compact calculators, numbered D1 to D6. Each one answers a single question, such as whether a price plan is affordable, or what an entry price implies in dollars. They sit between the book and the full model. The book explains the reasoning. The tools give a fast answer you can check by hand. The model puts everything together over five years.

## Scene 8. Conventions and honesty
On screen: Fictional inputs. Sourced external figures with caveats. Conflicts reported, not resolved. Pre-publication edition.

A few conventions apply throughout.

All default inputs in the workbook, and all SolaraPay figures, are illustrative. They were chosen to make the mechanics visible and to resemble the orders of magnitude found in the sector. They do not describe any real company. In the generic model, amounts are in local currency units. In the case, they are in shillings.

Where the course cites figures about real companies or about the sector, it says where they come from and how far they have been verified. Several published figures conflict with each other. When they do, the course reports the conflict rather than choosing the more convenient number.

The course follows the first edition of Book 2, issued as version 0.2 for pre-publication review. It will be revised after the workbook completes its test in Microsoft Excel, after the primary documents behind the claims marked as pending have been read and cited by page, and after practitioner review. Claims marked as pending or conflicting in Annex G of the book must not be quoted without checking the original source.

Nothing in the course is investment, legal, tax or accounting advice. Take professional advice in the jurisdiction concerned before acting on any of it.

## Scene 9. Recap and exercise
On screen: Exercise. Open the workbook. Checks sheet. Dashboard. Investment_Readiness: 2 of 23, STOP on a failed test.

To recap. The course asks whether PAYGo solar can become profitable and financeable. It treats each company as a retailer, a service provider and a lender at once, and it reads that company through cohorts and the receivables book. The book gives the reasoning, the workbook tests it on numbers, and the templates and decision tools carry it into a real transaction.

Your exercise before Module 1 is practical. Open MODEL 2 in its default state. Go first to the Checks sheet and confirm that it reads OK. Then open the Dashboard and note the peak equity and the investor internal rate of return, or IRR, for the Base case. Finally, open Investment_Readiness, confirm that it shows 2 of 23 gates passed and a decision of STOP on a failed test, and write down the name of the failed test and of two other gates that fail. You will return to that list at the end of the course.
