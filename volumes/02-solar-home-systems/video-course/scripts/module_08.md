# Module 8. Revenue recognition and credit losses

Duration: about 9 minutes. Book: Chapter 8. Model sheets: Cohort_T1 to Cohort_T5, FS, Annual, Credit_Portfolio, Credit_Assumptions, Products, Benchmark_Compare, Calibration, Investment_Readiness. Templates and tools: T01 Investment memo, T03 Customer credit policy.

## Learning objectives
1. Split a PAYGo contract into hardware revenue and financing income, and explain the judgements behind the split under IFRS 15.
2. Apply the IFRS 9 expected credit loss model to PAYGo receivables, including staging, the 30 and 90 day presumptions, write offs and repossession.
3. Follow one cohort through the workbook's simplified accounting, read ECL against financing revenue, and reconcile the management, auditor and lender views of profit.

## Scene 1. What profit is the company really making
On screen: Title "What profit am I really making?" with two judgements: revenue split, expected credit loss.

Every profit figure in a PAYGo plan, PAYGo meaning pay as you go, rests on two accounting judgements. The first is how much of each contract is a sale of hardware and how much is interest. The second is how much of the receivable the company expects never to collect, and when that expectation reaches the income statement.

Management, auditors and lenders often answer both differently, by enough to move a company from profit to loss.

## Scene 2. A sale with a financing component
On screen: Contract value split: hardware revenue at cash price on installation, financing income over the tenor.

Under IFRS 15, the International Financial Reporting Standard on revenue from contracts with customers, a PAYGo contract is usually a sale of a good with a significant financing component. The customer takes the device home on day one and pays more than the cash price over 12 to 48 months.

So the contract is split. Hardware revenue is recognised at the cash price when control passes, normally at installation. The difference between the total contract value and the cash price is financing income, recognised over the tenor. A company that books the whole contract on day one overstates revenue, and in a growing book it does so every year.

Judgements sit inside this. Title passes only on the final payment, but the common view is that the lockout protects the seller's security and control still passes on delivery. It is still a judgement. And the effective interest method front loads financing income compared with a straight line. Whether the practical expedient for contracts of one year or less applies to a 12 month Tier 1 contract is a matter to agree with the auditor.

## Scene 3. Expected credit losses under IFRS 9
On screen: Stage 1: 12 month ECL. Stage 2: lifetime ECL after a significant increase in credit risk. Stage 3: credit impaired. Presumptions at 30 and 90 days past due.

PAYGo receivables are held at amortised cost, and the allowance follows the IFRS 9 expected credit loss model, or ECL. Under the general approach, Stage 1 accounts carry a 12 month ECL. Stage 2 accounts, where credit risk has increased significantly since origination, carry a lifetime ECL. Stage 3 accounts are credit impaired, also at lifetime ECL.

Two rebuttable presumptions matter. A significant increase in credit risk is presumed beyond 30 days past due, and default no later than 90 days. Auditors tend to accept a rebuttal only with cohort evidence of cures.

There is a second debate. Some companies apply the simplified approach, with a lifetime allowance from day one and no staging. Others apply the general approach, treating the contracts as consumer loans in substance. Whatever the approach, ECL must reflect forward looking information, such as a drought or a currency shock.

## Scene 4. Write offs and repossession
On screen: Repossession rate by tier, from 0 per cent for Tier 1 to 70 per cent for Tier 5. SolaraPay observed loss given default about 99 per cent.

IFRS 9 requires a write off when there is no reasonable expectation of recovery, without setting a number of days.

Repossession is where PAYGo differs from unsecured lending. A recovered device goes into inventory at no more than its net realisable value, the resale price less refurbishment and selling costs. In the workbook, net recovery is defaults after a lag, times the repossession rate, times the resale share, times the cash price, less a recovery cost of 15 per cent of the proceeds. Repossession rates rise from zero for Tier 1 to 70 per cent for Tier 5.

The evidence often disappoints. The SolaraPay case, a fictional company with synthetic history, shows an observed loss given default of about 99 per cent. A plan that relies on resale values for Tiers 4 and 5 before the company has repossessed and resold a meaningful number is relying on a hope.

## Scene 5. How the workbook simplifies
On screen: Workbook conventions: hardware at cash price, financing income straight line, lifetime loss charged at sale, missed instalments written off as they fall due.

The AEF SHS PAYGo model simplifies these rules for transparency. Hardware revenue is the cash price at sale. Financing income is the PAYGo premium divided by the tenor, straight line. The allowance is set up at origination equal to the cohort's lifetime expected missed instalments, and consumed as they are missed. Missed instalments are written off as they fall due.

The whole lifetime loss hits the income statement at the sale, which is conservative. Straight line income is lower early than effective interest, also conservative. And the workbook's gross receivables and write offs are not comparable with audited figures, which will differ.

## Scene 6. One cohort, worked through
On screen: Tier 2 cohort of 1,000 units. Year 1: revenue 44.00 million, ECL charge 17.10 million. Year 2: revenue 8.00 million, no charge.

Take an illustrative Tier 2 cohort of one thousand units. Each has a cash price of thirty six thousand local currency units and a deposit of four thousand, so thirty two thousand is financed. The customer pays two thousand a month for 24 months. The contract is worth fifty two thousand, so the premium is sixteen thousand.

On the default curve, with a hazard of 2.6 per cent and a collection rate of 88 per cent, expected collections are thirty thousand eight hundred and ninety six per unit. Missed instalments are seventeen thousand one hundred and four, or 35.6 per cent of instalments due. The cohort's allowance at origination is seventeen point one million.

In Year 1 the cohort brings forty four million of revenue, thirty six million of hardware plus eight million of financing income, against the full seventeen point one million of credit cost. In Year 2 it brings eight million of revenue and no credit cost. A growing company's income statement always carries the full lifetime loss of the newest cohorts against only their first months of financing income.

Under the effective interest method, at about 3.53 per cent a month, financing income would be about eleven point three million in Year 1 and four point seven million in Year 2. The total is the same, the timing is not.

## Scene 7. Indicative ECL and the ratio to financing revenue
On screen: Credit_Portfolio sheet. Indicative ECL 323.6 million on 1,000 million of receivables, coverage 32.4 per cent. SolaraPay ECL to financing revenue 1.28 times against about 0.38 times.

The Credit_Portfolio sheet also computes an indicative stage based ECL as a diagnostic. Take a book of one billion local currency units, with eight hundred and twenty million current or up to 30 days late, ninety million in Stage 2 and ninety million in Stage 3. With Tier 2 inputs, a 30 per cent cure rate and an assumed resale share of 40 per cent, the loss factors are 22.85, 59.01 and 92.35 per cent. The indicative ECL is three hundred and twenty three point six million, a coverage of 32.4 per cent. SolaraPay reports 36.5 per cent in its latest month.

The calibrated SolaraPay plan shows an ECL charge of 1.28 times its financing revenue, against a reference of about 0.38 times. That reference is anecdotal and provisional. Part of the gap is mechanical. One Tier 2 cohort on the workbook's conventions has a lifetime ratio of 1.07 times and a first year ratio of 2.14 times, and a growing book sits above the lifetime figure. Part is credit, and part is pricing. The ratio is a good question and a poor verdict. Net credit losses against revenue, 37.5 per cent in Year 1 falling to 27.5 per cent in Year 5, answer it more directly.

## Scene 8. Three views of profit
On screen: Management view, auditor view, lender view, reconciled side by side.

Management tends to prefer judgements that smooth profit: a 12 month allowance on current accounts, a default definition beyond 90 days, optimistic recoveries and effective interest. Together they move credit cost later and income earlier.

The auditor tests whether those judgements are supportable.

The lender relies on neither. Its borrowing base counts eligible receivables at an advance rate and ignores the allowance. Its covenants test collections and arrears, not ECL. Changing ECL assumptions does not move the borrowing base by a single unit of currency. Present all three views side by side and reconcile them.
