# Module 5. Distribution and customer acquisition

Duration: about 9 minutes. Book: Chapter 5. Model sheets: Products, Inputs, Unit_Economics, Vintage_Input. Templates and tools: T04 Agent economics, D2 Unit economics calculator.

## Learning objectives
1. Build customer acquisition cost on a narrow and a fully loaded definition, and reconcile the two to the management accounts.
2. Explain why commission design and agent quality are credit variables, and read vintage curves cut by agent, channel and territory.
3. Recognise the data traces of fraud and ghost sales, and test acquisition cost together with credit in the unit economics.

## Scene 1. A customer is a credit decision
On screen: Title "What does a customer really cost?" with the line "A cheap customer who defaults in month four is the most expensive one".

The decision is what a customer really costs to acquire, and whether that cost is recovered before the customer stops paying.

In a PAYGo company, PAYGo meaning pay as you go, that is a credit question. The people who find customers also decide, in practice, who gets credit. A cheap customer who defaults in month four is the most expensive customer the company will ever sign.

## Scene 2. Who finds the customer
On screen: Three channels: direct sales agents, retail partners, installers.

PAYGo solar is sold through people. The typical customer is a rural household with a mobile money wallet and a seasonal income.

Three channels carry most of the work. Direct sales agents are the backbone, often paid entirely on commission. The company controls them only through the commission plan, the training and the data it watches.

Retail partners, such as agro dealers and phone shops, bring footfall. Their incentive is the sale, not the repayment.

Installers matter most for Tiers 3 to 5. When a customer says the system does not work, that often means, in collections language, that they are not going to pay. So installer quality shows up in early default rates as well as warranty costs.

## Scene 3. Commission structures
On screen: Flat commission, split commission, portfolio bonus, clawback.

The simplest plan pays a flat commission when the deposit is received and the system is activated. It is also the plan most likely to fill the book with accounts that never make a second payment.

The alternatives split the commission. Part is paid on activation and part later, if the account is still current at a checkpoint such as month three. The most direct form is the clawback, where commission already paid is recovered if the account defaults within a set window.

Each design has a cost. Deferred pay raises attrition among new agents. And where agents can simply leave for a competitor, a large clawback is a deterrent on paper only.

Here is a practical test for an investment committee. Ask what share of commission paid in the last twelve months was contingent on repayment. Then ask how much of it was actually withheld. If the second answer is close to zero, the contingency is not working.

## Scene 4. Fully loaded acquisition cost
On screen: Table from the book: narrow CAC of 3,260 per sale against fully loaded CAC of 6,060 per sale, Tier 2 territory, 300 sales a month.

The workbook defines customer acquisition cost, or CAC, as commission plus marketing per unit sold, entered by tier on the Products sheet. Most plans understate the true cost, because the field organisation sits in fixed overheads.

Take an illustrative territory selling a Tier 2 system, with figures in the local currency of the model's fictional market. Fifty agents each make six sales a month, so three hundred sales. Upfront commission is one thousand eight hundred local currency units a sale. A repayment linked commission of one thousand two hundred is paid on the 80 per cent of accounts still current at month three, which is nine hundred and sixty a sale. Marketing adds five hundred. The narrow CAC is three thousand two hundred and sixty a sale.

Now add the costs that exist only because the company is acquiring customers. Field supervisors add one thousand five hundred a sale. Recruitment and training to replace leavers add four hundred. Transport and fuel add seven hundred. Verification calls and fraud losses add two hundred. The fully loaded CAC is six thousand and sixty a sale, 1.86 times the narrow figure. On a cash price of thirty six thousand, that is about 16.8 per cent of the price, against 9.1 per cent on the narrow view.

Neither figure is wrong, as long as everyone knows which one is on the page. Ask for the reconciliation from the per unit figure to total sales and marketing expense in the management accounts. The gap is usually where the field organisation lives.

## Scene 5. Agent quality as a credit variable
On screen: Two cumulative repayment curves for the same product: top half of agents at 76 per cent, bottom half at 62 per cent at month 12.

Two agents in the same territory, selling the same product in the same month, can originate books that repay very differently.

Take a Tier 2 contract with an instalment of two thousand local currency units a month. By month twelve, twenty four thousand has fallen due. Suppose accounts from the top half of agents have repaid 76 per cent of that, and accounts from the bottom half 62 per cent. The gap of fourteen points is worth three thousand three hundred and sixty per account by month twelve alone. That is more than the entire narrow CAC, and it keeps growing in the second year.

So agent identity belongs in the cohort data. A credit officer should see vintage curves cut by agent, agent tenure, channel and territory. Full curves take two years to form, but early signals arrive within a quarter: deposit only accounts, accounts more than thirty days past due at month three, and systems never active for a full month.

## Scene 6. Fraud and ghost sales
On screen: Warning signs: shared phone numbers, shared wallets, month end bursts, devices never switched on.

Any plan that pays per activation invites fabricated activations. An agent registers a fictitious customer, pays the deposit from their own wallet and earns a commission larger than the deposit.

Most of this leaves traces. Deposit only accounts cluster by agent. Several accounts share a phone number or a wallet. Installations arrive in bursts just before the commission cut off. Some devices are never switched on.

The controls are routine: verification calls, location tagged installation photographs, separate deposit and commission wallets, and random field audits. Their cost belongs in fully loaded CAC. A company that shows no fraud losses at all usually has not looked.

For a lender, ghost sales are worse than ordinary defaults. A fictitious account looks current in its first month and counts as collateral. Eligibility criteria should exclude deposit only accounts and accounts without a first instalment.

## Scene 7. Opening new territories
On screen: Fixed cost per sale in a new territory: 9,000 at 100 sales, 4,500 at 200 sales, 3,000 at 300 sales.

New territories are expensive before they are productive. Suppose a new territory carries fixed costs of nine hundred thousand local currency units a month. At maturity it sells three hundred units a month, so those costs add three thousand a sale. In the first month, at one hundred sales, they add nine thousand. At two hundred sales they add four thousand five hundred.

Early cohorts in a new area also tend to repay worse. A plan that applies mature hazard rates to a new territory is optimistic twice, on cost and on credit. Treat it as a pilot, with a budget, a ramp and stop criteria.

## Scene 8. How CAC enters the unit economics
On screen: Unit_Economics sheet. SolaraPay LTV to CAC by tier: 2.4, 3.5, 5.5, 11.0 and 14.9 times.

On Unit_Economics, CAC is paid in full on the day of sale and recovered, if at all, in small daily payments. That timing is why it weighs so heavily on payback.

In the calibrated SolaraPay case, a fictional company, the ratio of lifetime value to CAC, or LTV to CAC, runs from 2.4 times for Tier 1 to 14.9 times for Tier 5. Cash payback runs from 9 to 22 months. The large systems look far better because acquisition cost does not rise with contract value. They also carry the least evidence, since Tiers 4 and 5 have no repayment history.

Payback must be read against survival. For Tier 2 at the default monthly hazard of 2.6 per cent, about 27 per cent of accounts have stopped paying by month twelve, and about 38 per cent by month eighteen. A Tier 2 payback of 17 months is recovered, on average, from the customers still paying.

## Scene 9. Recap and exercise
On screen: Template T04 Agent economics: narrow and fully loaded CAC, agent ranking, new territory sheet.

Acquisition cost is a credit variable, because the people who sell also select the borrowers. Measure CAC on both definitions and reconcile it to the accounts. Read vintage curves by agent and channel, and price fraud controls into the cost. Then test CAC and credit together in the unit economics.

Your exercise: open template T04 and enter the Tier 2 territory from this module. Confirm the fully loaded CAC of six thousand and sixty. Then cut the share of accounts qualifying for the deferred commission from 80 to 60 per cent and note how CAC per sale moves. In the workbook, move field costs from Inputs into the per unit acquisition line on Products, remove them from fixed costs, and check on Unit_Economics how LTV to CAC and payback change for each tier.
