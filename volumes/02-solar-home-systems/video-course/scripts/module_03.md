# Module 3. The regulatory checklist

Duration: about 8 minutes. Book: Chapter 3. Model sheets: Products, Inputs, Scenarios, Credit_Assumptions, Investment_Readiness. Templates and tools: T02 Investor due diligence checklist, D1 Price plan and APR calculator.

## Learning objectives
1. Name the rule categories that apply to a PAYGo company and the question to ask in each.
2. Translate each regulatory risk into the line of the model that moves, and test it through inputs and scenarios.
3. List the evidence an investor should expect, and decide which legal positions must be conditions precedent.

## Scene 1. What could stop the company
On screen: Title. "What could stop the company operating, or stop it scaling?"

This module supports one decision: what could stop the company operating, or stop it scaling.

A PAYGo, or pay as you go, company sits under several regimes at once. Consumer credit, payments, data protection, tax, product standards, environmental rules and exchange control. Each can change the economics. Some can halt the business.

The module is a checklist of rule categories. For each one you will see the question to ask, why it matters financially and what evidence to expect. It names no country's law and no regulator's rule. Rules differ by jurisdiction and change often. In every category, the position must be confirmed with local counsel before an investment committee relies on it.

## Scene 2. How to use the checklist
On screen: Licence: cost and timing. APR cap: price plan. Lockout ban: default hazard. Exchange control: debt service.

Regulatory diligence is often done late and narrowly. A better approach treats each rule as a driver of the financial model.

A licence requirement is a cost and a timing risk. A cap on the annual percentage rate, or APR, constrains the price plan. A ban on remote lockout changes the default hazard. An exchange control constrains dividends and debt service.

So the question is always the same. If the rule applies, or changes, which line of the model moves, and by how much? Where the answer is material, the committee should see the scenario run, not only the legal memo.

Three general points apply. The relevant law is that of each operating country. The perimeter around PAYGo is often unclear, because the product sits between a hire purchase sale, a consumer loan and a utility service. And unclear rules can become clear quickly, rarely in the company's favour.

## Scene 3. Credit licences and pricing
On screen: Is the contract credit? Who needs a licence? Disclosure. APR caps. Tier 2 example: flat 23.0%, effective 47.3%.

The first question is whether the PAYGo contract counts as credit under local law. If so, the company, or the entity holding the receivables, may need a lending, credit or hire purchase licence.

Without a required licence, contracts may be unenforceable, collections may be challenged and lenders' security may be impaired. With one come capital requirements and reporting. In a group, the same question applies to a financing subsidiary or a special purpose vehicle, an SPV.

Next comes pricing disclosure and APR caps. A company that has not computed its own APR will learn it from someone else. The illustrative Tier 2 plan has a flat rate of 23.0 per cent a year, but an effective annual rate of 47.3 per cent. SolaraPay's implied APRs run from 25 per cent on Tier 5 to 106 per cent on Tier 1.

A cap hits small, short products hardest. If Tier 1 breaches it, the company can raise the cash price, which shifts margin from financing income to hardware revenue without changing what the customer pays. Or it can accept a lower return, or withdraw the product. Model each option.

## Scene 4. Collection conduct and lockout
On screen: Lockout rules. Notice periods. Repossession. Agent incentives. Complaints log.

The lockout is the company's main collection tool. So ask whether there are rules on remote lockout, notice periods, repossession and conduct towards customers.

A rule that restricts disablement, or requires a grace period, changes the default hazard directly. A longer notice period before lockout will, other things equal, raise the share of customers who fall behind. A ban on repossession removes the recovery line altogether. In many portfolios that line is already small. SolaraPay's observed proxy for loss given default is about 99 per cent.

Conduct also carries licence and reputational risk. Aggressive collection by agents paid on recoveries can lead to sanctions, and to exclusion from results based financing, or RBF, and from development finance with consumer protection requirements.

Ask for the collections policy, the lockout procedures and the complaints log.

## Scene 5. Data and payments
On screen: Consent. Cross border transfer. Credit bureau. Mobile money fees. Settlement times.

Data protection asks what consent the company needs to use customer data, including mobile money histories and device usage, and whether data can cross borders. A restriction on using mobile money histories removes one of the best affordability sources. A limit on cross border transfer can affect a group that runs its credit platform from another country.

Credit bureau reporting cuts both ways. It can improve discipline and let good payers build a history that supports upgrades. It also brings compliance cost.

Almost all collections arrive through mobile money. In the workbook, operator fees are an input, as a percentage of cash collected. Settlement terms decide how quickly cash reaches the bank account, and so how much sits outside the borrowing base. Ask for every operator agreement, with fees, settlement times, exclusivity and termination clauses.

## Scene 6. Tax, standards and e-waste
On screen: VAT and duty on solar. Withholding. Transfer pricing. Tax 30%, losses carried forward. Product quality. Battery take back.

Many PAYGo cost structures rely on favourable VAT and duty treatment of solar products. Losing it raises the landed cost of every new unit. The workbook carries freight, duty and clearing as a percentage of the free on board cost, so you can run the change directly.

Withholding tax on interest raises the cost of offshore debt where the lender needs a gross up. On dividends, it cuts what the equity investor receives. Groups must also document transfer prices for hardware and services.

The workbook applies corporate tax at 30 per cent, with losses carried forward without limit. Confirm that locally. A cap on carry forward brings tax forward in a business that loses money in its early years.

Quality matters everywhere in the model. Customers stop paying for devices that do not work. Products that miss a standard may be blocked at import or excluded from RBF. Programmes that pay per verified connection, such as SEforALL's Universal Energy Facility, set their own eligibility conditions.

Batteries are hazardous at end of life. A take back obligation creates a cost that may fall years after the sale. For Tiers 4 and 5, with large lithium batteries, that cost is material.

## Scene 7. Exchange control and securitisation
On screen: Convert and remit: suppliers, debt, dividends, exit. 20% depreciation: IRR 46.4% to minus 16.0%. True sale opinion.

For an offshore investor, exchange control is often among the most important questions. Can the company convert local currency and remit it, for hardware, debt service, dividends and exit proceeds? If conversion is rationed or delayed, a company solvent in local currency may still fail to pay suppliers or lenders. Registration of foreign investment at entry is often a condition for repatriation later, so it cannot be left to exit.

The price risk is already severe. At default inputs, depreciation of 20 per cent a year turns a Base investor internal rate of return, or IRR, of 46.4 per cent into minus 16.0 per cent. Controls add a liquidity risk on top.

Securitisation lets a company fund its book at a scale a balance sheet cannot reach. Sun King is reported to have completed local currency securitisations in Kenya of about one hundred and thirty million dollars in May 2023, and about one hundred and fifty six million dollars in July 2025. That is company and secondary reporting. d.light is reported to have about seven hundred and eighteen million dollars of purchasing capacity across five facilities since 2020. That is secondary reporting, and it is purchasing capacity, not debt raised.

The legal questions are specific. Can receivables be assigned without customer consent? Does the sale survive the originator's insolvency? Can the originator keep servicing, including the lockout? In the workbook's default case, securitisation leaves the investor IRR almost unchanged, at 46.5 per cent against 46.4. Its value lies in scale, tenor and cost of funding.

## Scene 8. Recap and exercise
On screen: Exercise. T02 checklist. Regulatory map by category. Lockout restriction run on Products and Scenarios.

To recap. Treat every rule category as a driver of the model, and ask which line moves if the rule applies or changes. Expect evidence, not assertions, and have every position confirmed by local counsel. Where securitisation is in the funding plan, make a true sale opinion a condition precedent, not a post closing item.

Your exercise has two parts. First, using the legal and regulatory workstream of the T02 due diligence checklist, build a one page map for SolaraPay listing each category, its financial impact and the evidence still missing. Second, assume a new rule that delays lockout. Raise the Tier 1 and Tier 2 default hazards on the Products sheet by 30 per cent, and record the change in investor IRR and covenant breach months.
