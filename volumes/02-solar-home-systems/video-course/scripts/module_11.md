# Module 11. Funding the book: equity, debt and receivables facilities

Duration: about 8 minutes. Book: Chapter 11. Model sheets: Inputs, Products, Credit_Assumptions, Credit_Portfolio, Covenants, KPIs, Credit_Input, Vintage_Input, Sensitivity. Templates and tools: T07 Borrowing base and term sheet, T05 Lender KPI report, D4 Receivables financing calculator.

## Learning objectives
1. Match each layer of the funding stack to the evidence a PAYGo company can show at each stage, and explain the three jobs of equity.
2. Build a borrowing base from receivables by tier and DPD bucket, apply eligibility and a concentration limit, and measure how fast headroom disappears.
3. Judge which covenants test a growing PAYGo book well, and set out a lender's options on the SolaraPay facility.

## Scene 1. The funding stack by stage
On screen: Four stages: launch, early growth, scale, mature. Matching sources: equity and grants, hard currency term debt, local currency facilities, securitisation.

This module supports the decision of how growth should be financed: how much equity, which debt, in which currency and on what terms. The funding stack has to match the receivables book in size, tenor and currency.

Lenders lend against data. At launch, with a few cohorts, a PAYGo, or pay as you go, company raises equity, grants, results based financing and supplier credit. With 12 to 24 months of vintages it adds hard currency term debt from impact lenders. With full tenor cohorts and audited accounts it can raise local currency receivables facilities. Only with several years of vintages and a clean data tape can it sell receivables to a special purpose vehicle, or SPV.

A downturn can push a company back a stage. GOGLA reports off grid solar investment of about two hundred and ninety nine million dollars in 2024, down about 30 per cent, a figure from secondary reporting still to be checked against the primary source.

## Scene 2. What equity is for
On screen: Three jobs of equity: early losses, the unfunded part of the book, first loss. Peak equity: default and SolaraPay.

Equity does three jobs. It funds early losses. It funds the part of the book that lenders will not advance against. And it absorbs first loss on the whole balance sheet, which is what allows lenders to advance at all.

Founders tend to underestimate the third. A lender advancing 70 per cent relies on the other 30 per cent, and on the equity beyond it, to absorb losses first. When credit deteriorates, eligibility shrinks and the advance falls. The AEF SHS PAYGo model meets that shortfall with an automatic equity top up. For SolaraPay, the book's fictional company, peak equity is nine million dollars in the calibrated Base and forty one point nine million in the calibrated Severe case. An investor should know which figure it is underwriting.

## Scene 3. Hard currency term debt
On screen: SolaraPay term loan: three million dollars at 10 per cent. 405 million shillings at 135, 486 million at 162. Loss of 81 million shillings.

Dollar term loans from impact lenders and development finance institutions are often the first debt a company raises. They are patient and need no borrowing base. Their weakness is currency.

SolaraPay's three million dollar loan at 10 per cent, with a 12 month grace period and 36 months of amortisation, is four hundred and five million shillings at the opening rate of 135 per dollar. If the rate moves to 162, a 20 per cent rise in the dollar, the principal is four hundred and eighty six million. The company has lost eighty one million shillings before repaying anything. Use such debt for early losses and inventory, not for the book, and move the book to local currency as soon as you can.

## Scene 4. How a receivables facility works
On screen: Facility terms: advance rates 50, 70 and 75 per cent, eligibility to 30 DPD, concentration limits, cash sweep.

A receivables facility is a revolving loan secured on PAYGo receivables, usually in local currency. The amount available each month is the borrowing base: advance rate times eligible receivables, by product, less deductions for concentration. The model's default advance rates are 50 per cent for Tier 1, 70 per cent for Tiers 2 and 3 and 75 per cent for Tiers 4 and 5.

Eligibility decides what counts at all. The model's default is up to 30 days past due, or DPD. Lenders add criteria such as a lockable device and a verified customer, each needing an auditable data field. Concentration limits stop the base drifting towards assets the lender knows least about. Under a cash sweep, collections pass through a controlled account, and cash above an agreed minimum repays the facility first.

## Scene 5. A worked borrowing base
On screen: T07 borrowing base certificate. Gross book 2,950, eligible 2,500, base before limits 1,746, concentration deduction 135, base 1,611, drawn 1,500, headroom 111. Figures in millions of shillings.

Take an illustrative book, in millions of shillings. The gross book is two thousand nine hundred and fifty. Receivables current or up to 30 days past due are eligible, two thousand five hundred in all, about 85 per cent. Applying the advance rate tier by tier gives a base of one thousand seven hundred and forty six.

Now a concentration limit. Tiers 4 and 5 have less than twelve months of history and may not exceed 12 per cent of the eligible pool, a cap of three hundred. They contribute four hundred and eighty, so one hundred and eighty is excess. At a 75 per cent advance, the deduction is one hundred and thirty five. The borrowing base is one thousand six hundred and eleven.

Against a facility limit of two thousand and a drawn balance of one thousand five hundred, headroom is one hundred and eleven. The limit is not the constraint. The base is. And the effective advance on the gross book is about 55 per cent, well below every headline rate.

## Scene 6. How quickly headroom disappears
On screen: Migration of 100 million of Tier 2 receivables past 30 DPD. Headroom falls from 111 to 32.

Suppose one hundred million of Tier 2 receivables slip past 30 days, and nothing else changes. Tier 2's contribution to the base falls by seventy. The eligible pool shrinks, so the concentration cap falls to two hundred and eighty eight, and the deduction on Tiers 4 and 5 rises to one hundred and forty four. The base falls to one thousand five hundred and thirty two, and headroom from one hundred and eleven to thirty two.

A migration of about 3.4 per cent of the book has removed more than 70 per cent of headroom. Another such month would breach the base and force repayment from collections that are themselves weakening.

## Scene 7. Covenants that fit a growing book
On screen: Covenants sheet defaults: collection rate 70 per cent, receivables at risk 15 per cent, 30 plus DPD 25 per cent, 90 plus DPD 18 per cent, headroom zero, debt to equity 3.0 times, DSCR 1.20 times.

The model's default covenants are a trailing three month collection rate of at least 70 per cent, receivables at risk of no more than 15 per cent, 30 plus DPD of no more than 25 per cent, 90 plus DPD of no more than 18 per cent, non negative headroom, debt to book equity of no more than 3.0 times, and an annual DSCR, the debt service coverage ratio, of at least 1.20 times. Definitions should follow the current published PAYGo PERFORM documents, the framework developed by CGAP, GOGLA and Lighting Global with other partners.

DSCR is ill suited to a growing book. Lending to new customers runs through operating cash flow, so coverage falls when growth accelerates, whatever the book's quality. SolaraPay's calibrated Base sits below 1.20 times in Years 1 to 4 while every portfolio covenant holds. Portfolio covenants with a cash sweep work better. A cure period, often one or two test dates, should be long enough for a collections plan to show results. Equity cures suit leverage covenants, not collection covenants, because new equity does not change how customers pay.

## Scene 8. SolaraPay from the lender's side
On screen: SolaraPay request: 4.5 billion shilling facility at 16 per cent. Lowest projected trailing collection rate 70.0 per cent. History: 77.4, then 69.3, latest month 66.5 per cent. PAR30 17.0 per cent.

SolaraPay asks a local bank for a four point five billion shilling facility at 16 per cent, with the default advance rates and eligibility to 30 days. On the calibrated projection it passes every monthly covenant, but only just. Debt to book equity peaks at 2.51 times in Year 3, against a limit of 3.0 times.

The weak point is collections. The lowest projected trailing collection rate is exactly 70.0 per cent, so no headroom. The history is worse: 77.4 per cent in the first year, 69.3 per cent in the second, and 66.5 per cent in the latest month. PAR30, the share of the portfolio more than 30 days past due, is 17.0 per cent and rising.

A lender has two sound options. One is to set the covenant at a level the book can meet, such as 65 per cent on a trailing three month basis, with a cure period and a written collections plan. The other is to defer the facility until the trend turns. Lending at 70 per cent and waiving the breach in month one is not a third option.

## Scene 9. Recap and exercise
On screen: Recap: equity carries first loss, the borrowing base binds, portfolio covenants over DSCR. Exercise: T07 borrowing base certificate.

The funding stack follows the evidence, and equity carries the first loss that makes debt possible. The borrowing base binds, and small migrations past 30 days can erase headroom. Portfolio covenants test a growing book better than DSCR, and SolaraPay's collection covenant must be set where the book can actually perform.

Open the T07 borrowing base certificate on its worked example and confirm the base of one thousand six hundred and eleven and headroom of one hundred and eleven. Then move Tier 2 receivables past 30 days in steps of fifty million, and record the step at which headroom turns negative.
