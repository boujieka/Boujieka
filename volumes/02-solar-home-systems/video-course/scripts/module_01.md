# Module 1. The PAYGo business: retailer, utility and lender

Duration: about 9 minutes. Book: Chapter 1. Model sheets: Dashboard, KPIs, Unit_Economics, Credit_Portfolio, Vintage_Dashboard, Checks, Investment_Readiness. Templates and tools: T08 PAYGo business model canvas, D2 Unit economics calculator.

## Learning objectives
1. Separate a PAYGo company into its retail, service and lending businesses and name the numbers that belong to each.
2. Reconcile the day one margin of a single sale with its cash flow and its lifetime accounting result.
3. Explain why cohorts and the receivables book, not the sales line, are the unit of analysis, and recognise the three common failure modes.

## Scene 1. The decision
On screen: Title. "What business is a PAYGo company really in?"

This module supports one decision, which comes before any other. What business is a PAYGo, or pay as you go, solar company really in? And therefore, which numbers should an investor, a lender or a board read first?

Funding follows analysis. A company analysed as a retailer will be funded as a retailer. And a retailer's funding will not carry a two year consumer credit book.

## Scene 2. Three businesses in one legal entity
On screen: Three panels. Retailer: gross margin. Service provider: cost to serve. Lender: receivables and credit losses.

The customer pays a deposit, takes the system home and pays a daily rate through mobile money for a fixed tenor. At the end, the device unlocks for good. Seen from outside, that is one business. Inside, it is three.

The first is a retailer. It buys hardware, runs agents and earns a gross margin.

The second is a utility like service provider. For two to four years it serves each active account, at a cost largely independent of the original sale.

The third is a consumer finance company. Every unit sold creates a receivable, in effect an unsecured loan to a household with irregular income. It must fund that book.

Read as a retailer, the business looks highly profitable. Read as a lender, it looks like a high risk portfolio. Each reading is partly right. The investment case depends on how the three interact.

## Scene 3. The lockout is not security
On screen: Lockout. Daily repayment decision. Weakens late in tenor. No recovery value. SolaraPay loss given default about 99%.

The lockout does not secure the receivable the way a charge over an asset secures a loan. It gives the customer a reason to keep paying, every few days. Collection behaviour becomes visible quickly. The deterrent weakens as the tenor ends and the battery ages. And the lockout creates no recovery value. The company recovers money only if it can retrieve, refurbish and resell the device for more than that costs. In the SolaraPay case, the observed proxy for loss given default is about 99 per cent.

At unlock the customer becomes an owner. Many companies cannot evidence how many reach ownership, and the workbook will not invent that number.

## Scene 4. One sale, two stories
On screen: Worked example, Tier 2 SHS with TV. Cash price 40,000. Deposit 4,000. Daily rate 72. Tenor 24 months. Landed cost 22,000. Acquisition cost 4,000.

Take one illustrative sale of a solar home system with a television in the fictional model market.

The cash price is forty thousand local currency units. The deposit is four thousand. The daily rate is seventy two, which gives a monthly instalment of seventy two times 365 divided by twelve, or two thousand one hundred and ninety. Over twenty four months that is fifty two thousand five hundred and sixty of scheduled instalments. With the deposit, the contract is worth fifty six thousand five hundred and sixty. The PAYGo premium, the financing charge, is sixteen thousand five hundred and sixty. The hardware costs twenty two thousand landed, and acquiring the customer costs four thousand.

On the income statement, day one looks good. Hardware revenue of forty thousand, less cost of sales of twenty two thousand, gives a gross margin of eighteen thousand, or 45 per cent. After acquisition cost, the contribution is fourteen thousand. Financing income of six hundred and ninety a month follows over the tenor.

In cash, day one looks very different. The company has paid twenty two thousand for hardware and four thousand for acquisition, and received four thousand of deposit. It is twenty two thousand local currency units out of pocket.

## Scene 5. The repayment curve
On screen: Chart. Cumulative cash per unit by month. Minus 22,000 at month 0, crossing zero at month 14, 11,832 at month 24.

The workbook drives repayment with two parameters. The monthly default hazard, 2.6 per cent for Tier 2, is the share of paying accounts that stop for good each month. The collection rate on paying accounts, here 88 per cent, reflects the days even good customers miss.

Over the full tenor, expected collections come to thirty three thousand eight hundred and thirty two. That is 64.4 per cent of the scheduled instalments.

The unit recovers its cash outlay in month fourteen. Over the tenor it returns eleven thousand eight hundred and thirty two in cash. And that is before mobile money fees, servicing, warranty and the cost of funding a deficit for more than a year.

## Scene 6. Where accounting and cash meet
On screen: Day one result with provision: a loss of 4,728. Lifetime result: 11,832. Without provision: 14,000 profit on day one.

Expected missed instalments over the contract are fifty two thousand five hundred and sixty less thirty three thousand eight hundred and thirty two. That is eighteen thousand seven hundred and twenty eight.

The workbook charges that amount as a loss allowance at origination. So the day one result is eighteen thousand, less four thousand, less eighteen thousand seven hundred and twenty eight. That is a loss of four thousand seven hundred and twenty eight. Financing income of sixteen thousand five hundred and sixty then brings the lifetime result to eleven thousand eight hundred and thirty two. Accounting and cash reconcile.

The workbook's treatment is a stated simplification of the international standards, IFRS 15 and IFRS 9, chosen because it makes each cohort's accounting and cash agree exactly. This is an analytical modelling treatment and does not constitute a determination of the applicable accounting treatment under IFRS.

A company that recognises losses only as accounts fall behind reports fourteen thousand of contribution on day one. When sales grow fast, margin on new sales dominates, and losses belong to older, smaller cohorts. Reported profit looks healthy. Cash is still minus twenty two thousand per unit. A company selling three thousand such units a month needs sixty six million local currency units of new funding every month.

That is the gap. Everything that matters in the business sits in it.

## Scene 7. Cohorts and the receivables book
On screen: Cohort curves against plan at the same age. Receivables book: largest asset, collateral, main use of equity. Vintage_Dashboard.

Any month's results blend cohorts of different ages. A falling portfolio collection rate can reflect weaker credit, faster growth, or both. Without cohort data you cannot tell which.

So the course treats the cohort, or vintage, as the primary object of analysis. A cohort is the contracts originated in the same month, by tier. Its repayment curve, read against plan at the same age, is the most direct evidence that underwriting works. The receivables book is the company's largest asset and the collateral for its debt.

PAYGo PERFORM is the industry's reporting standard. Its current version, the technical guide published by GOGLA in June 2026, defines five KPIs, or key performance indicators, on repayment and ownership. They are computed by the company on its own contract data. The workbook keeps the older ratios, such as the collection rate, as operational and lender metrics, each with its definition printed, and reports PERFORM 2026 figures only when the company supplies them. The sector evidence is sobering, although it still has to be read in the original. The ESMAP Off-Grid Solar Market Trends Report 2024 is reported to give an average PAYGo collection rate of about 62 per cent for 2021 to 2023. That figure is pending the primary document, and it is a collection rate, not a PERFORM repayment rate.

## Scene 8. How PAYGo companies fail
On screen: Three failure modes. Growth outruns funding. Credit drift. Foreign exchange.

The failure modes are few, and they compound.

The first is growth outrunning funding. In the workbook's default case, the fictional company needs peak equity of ten million dollars. In the Severe case that rises to forty six point six million dollars.

The second is credit drift. In the SolaraPay case, the portfolio collection rate fell from 77.4 per cent in the first history year to 69.3 per cent in the second.

The third is foreign exchange, or FX, because companies borrow in dollars and collect in local currency. In the default sensitivities, depreciation of 20 per cent a year turns a Base investor internal rate of return, or IRR, of 46.4 per cent into minus 16.0 per cent. Freezing prices on new contracts cuts it to 7.9 per cent.

None of these shows first on the income statement. The sector has seen distress. BBOXX LTD, a UK company, is recorded as having entered administration on 19 May 2025. The Gazette notice and the register entry have not yet been read, so the date is pending the primary document. The course does not speculate on the causes.

## Scene 9. Recap and exercise
On screen: Exercise. T08 canvas. Three businesses. Day one margin against cash. Unit_Economics and D2.

To recap. A PAYGo company is a retailer, a service provider and a lender in one entity. Its day one margin and its cash diverge for more than a year per unit, and the gap grows with growth. Read the cohort curves, the funding plan and the FX bridge before the income statement.

Your exercise uses the T08 PAYGo business model canvas. Fill it for a company you know, or for SolaraPay, with gross margin, cost to serve per active account and credit losses in separate blocks. Then open Unit_Economics in the workbook, or the D2 unit economics calculator, and find the month in which each tier recovers its cash outlay. Write down which tier takes longest, and how much funding each unit needs until then.
