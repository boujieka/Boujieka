# Part VI | The public-finance layer

## Chapter 13. Direct support and contingent liabilities

### Two kinds of public commitment

The state supports a hydro project in two ways. Direct support is cash it actually spends: equity in a project company or state utility, capital grants and viability gap payments, the cost of building the transmission line, and budget transfers to the utility. Contingent liabilities are payments it may have to make if something happens: a guaranteed loan defaults, the utility misses PPA payments covered by a sovereign guarantee, the concession is terminated, or the central bank cannot provide foreign exchange.

The literature on this distinction is long. Polackova's 1998 paper is still a good introduction to the idea that contingent liabilities are a hidden form of fiscal risk [@FR-14]. More recent work has measured what happens when they materialise. Using a dataset of realised contingent liabilities, Bova and co-authors estimated an average fiscal cost of about 6 percent of GDP per episode; the breakdown by type, about 1.2 percent for PPPs and 3 percent for SOEs, we saw only in a summary and could not confirm in the full text [@FR-13].

### Multilateral guarantees are sovereign exposure

It is common to hear that a World Bank or African Development Bank guarantee "de-risks" a project. It does, for the lenders. For the state, a guarantee from a multilateral bank to commercial lenders is backed by an indemnity from the government to the multilateral bank. Uganda signed an indemnity agreement with IDA on 18 July 2007 to support the Bujagali partial risk guarantee of up to USD 115 million [@A2:S53]. Cameroon backs the IBRD payment guarantee of up to EUR 86 million and loan guarantee of up to EUR 171 million for Nachtigal through a similar indemnity [@A2:S3]. The guarantee changes the lender's counterparty from the utility or the state to a triple-A institution. It leaves the state's obligation in place.

That is not an argument against such guarantees. They lengthen tenor, lower pricing and bring in lenders who would otherwise stay away, and those benefits can far exceed the cost. It is an argument for counting them.

### Three measures, kept apart

The model reports three measures of contingent exposure and does not add them together.

The first is the maximum simultaneous exposure in each year:

$$ X^max^~t~ = max( TERM~t~ , DG~t~ + PG~t~ ) + FXG~t~ $$ (13.1)

where *TERM* is the termination amount, *DG* the guaranteed share of senior debt, *PG* twelve months of PPA payments under a sovereign payment guarantee and *FXG* foreign exchange cover on remittances. Termination and the debt and payment guarantees are not added because termination would replace them: once the state has bought out the project, it no longer guarantees its debt or its PPA.

The second is the set of calls the model actually computes in the active scenario: budget backstop payments, guarantee calls and minimum-revenue payments. These are deterministic. In the offtaker stress, they are the mechanism by which the utility's gap becomes a fiscal cost.

The third is an expected loss, computed as user-entered annual call probabilities times exposure times loss given call. We include it because ministries are often asked for a single number, and we label it clearly as a judgement. The probabilities in the model are not calibrated default rates, and the expected loss is only as good as they are. A number presented as an expected value acquires an authority it has not earned. We would rather show the maximum exposure and the scenario calls and let the reader judge the likelihood.

In the base case the state commits government equity, a 5 percent viability gap contribution and the full cost of the transmission line, and receives tax, royalties and dividends in return. Peak annual cash need is about {{m.fis_peak_rev|pct1}} of government revenue, in the construction years. The stresses show how thin that margin is. The offtaker stress and the currency step both turn the fiscal NPV deeply negative, as Chapter 8 showed, and they do so without any change in the project's own debt cover.

## Chapter 14. Project-level fiscal exposure screening

### What a screen can and cannot do

The IMF and World Bank Debt Sustainability Framework for Low-Income Countries sets thresholds for debt burden indicators according to a country's debt-carrying capacity. For the present value of public and publicly guaranteed external debt, the thresholds are 30, 40 and 55 percent of GDP for weak, medium and strong capacity; the corresponding benchmarks for total public debt are 35, 55 and 70 percent [@FR-09]. The framework also includes a contingent liability stress test, with a default shock of 2 percent of GDP for SOE debt and 35 percent of the PPP capital stock where that stock exceeds 3 percent of GDP [@FR-09]. PFRAM 2.0 is the standard tool for assessing the fiscal costs and risks of individual PPPs and portfolios [@FR-01; @FR-02].

The screen in the companion model does not replace either. It answers a narrower question that ministries face in practice, often under time pressure: does this one project add material fiscal pressure, and should it be escalated to the debt management office before signature?

### Four tests

The screen applies four tests:

1. Does the project's on-budget debt, plus cumulative fiscal outlays, add more than 1 percent of GDP at its peak?
2. Does peak contingent exposure exceed 2 percent of GDP?
3. Does the peak annual cash requirement exceed 1 percent of government revenue?
4. Does the project push public debt above the user's benchmark when it was below it before?

The results are combined with the latest published DSA risk rating. The screen returns low pressure when no test is breached and the rating is low or moderate, high pressure when three or more tests are breached or when any test is breached in a country already at high risk or above its debt benchmark, and moderate pressure otherwise. All thresholds are editable; the defaults are screening conventions that we chose, not official limits.

### Lumora

In the base case, Lumora breaches two tests: peak contingent exposure of about {{m.sc_cl|pct1}} of GDP and peak cash requirement of about {{m.sc_cash|pct1}} of revenue. With Navaria's fictional debt at 48 percent of GDP and a moderate DSA rating, the screen returns moderate pressure. The low case, the 27 percent cost overrun, the offtaker stress and the currency step all move it to high.

## Chapter 15. Who bears what

### The allocation matrix

The model maps sixteen risks against eight parties: government, developer, EPC contractor, lender, utility, consumer, insurer and development finance institution. For each risk it records the primary bearer, the parties that share it, whether it can be mitigated by contract, the main instruments, and the model stress that quantifies it. ### Instruments and where they lead

The instruments available to mitigate payment and political risk are well known. MIGA offers cover against the non-honouring of sovereign financial obligations [@PF-03]. The World Bank offers loan and payment guarantees [@PF-01; @PF-02]. The African Development Bank provides partial risk guarantees, as for the Sahofika project in Madagascar [@PF-06]. African Trade and Investment Development Insurance runs a regional liquidity support facility that, according to its published description, can cover up to twelve months of revenue [@PF-04].

Almost all of them move risk towards the state or towards a multilateral institution that the state indemnifies. A liquidity facility is a partial exception, because it bridges temporary shortfalls rather than absorbing permanent ones. That is why the matrix counts how many risks end with the government as primary bearer. In the Lumora PPP, it is the largest single bearer of risk, ahead of the developer.

## Chapter 16. The Hydropower Bankability Framework

### Design principles

We built the framework around four principles.

It should be explainable. Each gate is a short list of tests, each test is a named metric, and each metric has three visible thresholds that separate ready, conditional, development gap and critical gap.

It should be auditable. Every metric is a cell in the model, and every threshold is an input that a reviewer can see and change.

It should have no hidden weights. A gate takes the status of its weakest test, and the overall verdict follows from the weakest gate. Weighted scores look precise and hide trade-offs that should be argued over. A project with excellent hydrology and an insolvent offtaker is not "70 percent bankable".

It should be honest about its thresholds. We set them as screening values and say so. They should be calibrated to the lender term sheet, the country's fiscal rules and the project's context.

### The gates

Table: Table 16.1. The nine gates and their tests
| Gate | Tests |
|---|---|
| 1. Resource | Years of reliable flow record; P90 to P50 ratio; maturity of the hydrology study |
| 2. Demand | Bankable to contracted demand in the worst of the first five years; project share of system peak |
| 3. Configuration | Unit cost against benchmark; capacity factor; maturity of the feasibility study |
| 4. Transmission | Evacuation capacity at commercial operation against installed capacity; transmission lag; transmission financing secured |
| 5. Offtaker and utility | Payment capacity against PPA bill in the worst of the first ten years; collection rate; months of payment security |
| 6. Regulation and PPA | Regulatory items at critical gap; items at gap; fixed share of revenue |
| 7. Financing | Minimum DSCR; financing gap against total uses; equity return against target |
| 8. Public finance | Peak cash need against revenue; peak contingent exposure against GDP; DSA rating |
| 9. Environment and society | ESIA compliance; resettlement status; transboundary issues |

A test scores 3 when it meets the ready threshold, 2 when it meets the conditional threshold, 1 when it meets the development-gap threshold and 0 otherwise. The overall verdict is "not bankable" if any gate is at critical gap, "not yet bankable" if any is at development gap, "bankable subject to conditions" if any is conditional, and "ready for financial close" only if every gate is ready.

### Reading the table

Three patterns stand out.

The project's own metrics are robust. Across every stress, minimum DSCR stays above 1.5 and the project never misses debt service, because the two-part tariff, deemed energy and the budget backstop protect it. A lender looking only at the project would see a resilient credit.

The utility and the state are not robust. The stresses that matter most for them, offtaker weakness and currency, leave the project untouched and move hundreds of millions to more than a billion dollars of present value onto the public balance sheet.

Construction risk is shared. Overrun and delay hurt the sponsors through a lower equity return and a larger financing gap, and the state through larger public contributions and higher contingent exposure.

The conclusion is the thesis of the book in numerical form. A project can be bankable on its own terms while the arrangements that make it bankable leave the state carrying risks it has not priced.

## Chapter 19. Rules for financial close

The rules below are our synthesis of the evidence and the modelling in this book. They are written for the people who negotiate and approve hydro transactions, and they are deliberately blunt.

1. Price and size on P90, and also test a run of dry years. A single-year statistic says nothing about persistence, and persistence is what breaks debt service.
2. Budget against the reference class. A median real overrun of 27 percent is an expectation, not a stress [@HY-08].
3. Finance the line with the plant. Same close, same timetable, and an interface agreement that says who pays deemed energy and for how long.
4. Compute the buyer's maximum sustainable payment before negotiating the tariff. If the bill exceeds it, decide openly who will fund the difference.
5. Match currencies where you can. Local-currency tranches, partial local-currency tariffs or explicit sharing of devaluation risk are cheaper than a guarantee called after a crisis.
6. Treat every guarantee as debt that has not yet arrived. Record it, cap it and disclose it, as the PPP disclosure framework and the IMF fiscal transparency code recommend [@FR-04; @FR-08].
7. Tie every grant to a lower tariff or a cap on returns. Otherwise public money becomes private profit.
8. Look at the portfolio, not only the project. One PPA may pass the screen while the sum of all of them does not. The Lao experience is the warning [@INT:S13].
9. Procure competitively and in the open. It decides who will lend, and on what terms.
10. Close only when no gate is at critical gap and the Ministry of Finance has signed off the fiscal screen in writing.
