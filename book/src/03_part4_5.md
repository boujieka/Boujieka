# Part IV | The offtaker and the contract

## Chapter 8. The utility and the maximum sustainable PPA payment

### The assumption most models make

A standard project finance model has a revenue line, and the revenue line assumes the buyer pays. Sensitivity tables may haircut generation or tariff, but the counterparty's ability to pay is treated as a credit question for someone else. In Sub-Saharan Africa that is a dangerous habit.

The World Bank's work on utility finances gives the reasons. Transmission and distribution losses across the region averaged about 15 percent of energy dispatched, and 23 percent excluding South Africa, against a benchmark of about 10 percent. Bill collection ranged from about 58 percent in the weakest utilities to close to 100 percent in the best [@UT-02]. In Kojima and Trimble's 39-country assessment, only two power sectors were found to be financially viable and about half covered their operating costs [@UT-01]. (We saw these two figures in a summary rather than the full report, and they should be checked against it.) The case files repeat the theme at project level. Ghana's ECG, for example, owed the Bui Power Authority about USD 612 million in March 2023 [@A1:S40].

### A cash model of the offtaker

The companion model builds a simplified cash model of the utility. All local-currency flows are translated into US dollars at the projected exchange rate, so that the utility's ability to pay can be compared directly with a dollar PPA bill.

$$ CASH^pre^~t~ = COLL~t~ + TR~t~ - OPEX~t~ - OS~t~ - EDS~t~ $$ (8.1)

Collections *COLL* are sales times the retail tariff divided by the exchange rate, times the collection rate. *TR* is government transfers, *OPEX* the utility's own operating costs, *OS* the cost of supply from sources other than the new plant, and *EDS* debt service on existing borrowing. Because the new plant displaces other supply, *OS* falls when it comes online, and the utility gets credit for that saving.

From this we define the maximum sustainable PPA payment:

$$ MSP~t~ = max(0, CASH^pre^~t~) / c $$ (8.2)

where *c* is a coverage buffer, 1.2 by default. The buffer represents the share of free cash a prudent utility, or its regulator, would not commit to a single supplier. The offtaker payment capacity gap is then

$$ GAP~t~ = max(0, PPA~t~ - MSP~t~) $$ (8.3)

where *PPA~t~* is the utility's share of the project's billed revenue.

### Where the gap goes

The model sends the gap through three doors in a fixed order. If the government has committed to backstop the utility from the budget, the gap becomes a fiscal transfer. If not, and the structure includes a sovereign payment guarantee, the gap becomes a guarantee call. If neither applies, the gap becomes arrears owed to the project, which reduce its cash revenue and therefore its debt cover.

$$ BACKSTOP~t~ = b × GAP~t~,   CALL~t~ = (1 - b) × g × GAP~t~,   UNPAID~t~ = GAP~t~ - BACKSTOP~t~ - CALL~t~ $$ (8.4)

with *b* and *g* equal to one when a backstop or a guarantee exists. The sequence is a modelling choice. In practice governments sometimes pay late rather than not at all, and guarantees often cover only a fixed number of months. The point of the routing is to make the destination visible, not to predict the exact timing.

### What breaks the utility

In the Lumora base case the utility can pay. Its payment capacity is about {{base_locked.ut_ratio10|x1}} times the PPA bill in the worst of the first ten operating years, and there is no gap.

Two stresses change that.

The first is an offtaker stress. From commercial operation, collections fall by 8 percentage points, government transfers are cut by 30 percent and the retail tariff is frozen in nominal terms for five years before indexation resumes. Payment capacity falls to zero within the first ten years. Over the life of the PPA the cumulative gap is about USD {{offtaker.rev_govreq|n0}} million in nominal terms. With the budget backstop in place, the fiscal NPV of the project to the state moves from about USD {{base_locked.fis_npv|n0}} million to about USD {{offtaker.fis_npv|n0}} million.

The second is a one-off devaluation of 50 percent at commercial operation. The PPA is in dollars, retail tariffs are in local currency and indexation does not catch up with the step. Payment capacity falls to {{fx.ut_ratio10|x2}} of the bill, the lifetime gap is about USD {{fx.rev_govreq|n0}} million and fiscal NPV is about USD {{fx.fis_npv|n0}} million.

!fig|book/figures/fig8_1_utility.png|Figure 8.1. Utility PPA bill against maximum sustainable payment, base case and offtaker stress (USD million, nominal). The shaded area is the offtaker payment capacity gap. Source: companion model, full-engine runs.

The numbers are large because the mechanism compounds. In the fictional base case, the utility's cash margin before the new PPA is a modest share of its collections. A permanent price shock, whether a tariff freeze or a devaluation without pass-through, lowers that margin every year for thirty years. The project's lenders see none of this, because in these runs the state pays. This is the mechanism by which a project that is bankable on its own terms becomes a fiscal problem, and it is invisible in any model that assumes the offtaker pays.

### Limits of the utility model

The utility model is deliberately simple. It has no balance sheet, it treats the other supply mix as half dollar-linked, and it lets operating costs scale with sales volume. A real assessment should start from audited accounts and a tariff path agreed with the regulator. The IMF's SOE health-check and stress-test tools provide a fuller framework for that work [@FR-11; @FR-12]. What the simple model does well is make the question unavoidable: before negotiating the tariff, compute what the buyer can pay.

## Chapter 9. Regulation as an input to bankability

### A readiness screen, not a legal opinion

The model's regulatory matrix scores sixteen items as ready, partial, gap or critical gap. The items are the electricity law, the generation licence, water rights, the concession or implementation agreement, PPP legislation, the IPP procurement framework, enforceability of the PPA, tariff regulation, regulator independence, grid access, the grid code, environmental and social permits, land rights, foreign exchange convertibility, the tax regime and the rules on repatriation.

The matrix is a development screen. It asks whether each element is in a state that lenders' counsel would accept, not whether it is good law. Gate 6 counts the items at gap and critical gap and converts the counts into a status.

### Governance decides who lends

The case files suggest that governance and procurement failures stop more projects than technical ones. At Gibe III in Ethiopia, the main civil works contract was awarded without competitive tender, which was incompatible with World Bank procurement policy; the African Development Bank and the European Investment Bank withdrew in 2010, and the electro-mechanical package was financed by ICBC [@A1:S53; @A1:S54]. The first attempt at Bujagali in Uganda collapsed after the export credit agencies withdrew in January 2002, a month after Board approval, and corruption investigations involving an EPC contractor followed [@A2:S48]. At Batoka Gorge, Zambia withdrew from the 2019 arrangement in June 2023, citing improper procurement, and the project is being re-tendered [@A2:S57].

We draw a modest conclusion from this. Transparent, competitive procurement is part of bankability. It decides which lenders can participate, which in turn decides tenor, pricing and the guarantees the state is asked to provide.

### Environment and society

Gate 9 covers environmental and social readiness: compliance of the ESIA and management plans with lender standards such as the IFC Performance Standards or the Hydropower Sustainability Standard [@HY-03], the status of resettlement, and transboundary issues. The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam shows that transboundary questions can outlast construction; at the time of our research there was still no agreement with Egypt and Sudan on filling and operation [@A1:S1]. Lenders bound by safeguard policies treat these questions as conditions, not as afterthoughts.

## Chapter 10. The PPA: who carries water, volume, currency and termination risk

### The two-part tariff

Most African hydro IPPs use some version of a two-part tariff. A capacity or availability charge, in dollars per kW per month, pays for fixed costs, debt service included, as long as the plant is available. An energy charge, in dollars per MWh, pays for energy delivered. The larger the capacity share, the less hydrology risk the IPP carries and the more the buyer carries.

Lumora's capacity charge is USD 26 per kW per month and its energy charge USD 25 per MWh, in 2026 dollars, which puts about {{m.rev_fixed|pct0}} of lifetime revenue on the capacity side. That is why the drought stress in Chapter 3 barely touched the equity return. The protection is not free. In a drought the utility pays capacity charges for a plant that produces less, and it buys replacement energy elsewhere, often from thermal plants at a higher cost.

### Volume clauses

Deemed-energy clauses pay for energy that the seller could have delivered but the buyer or the grid could not take. Take-or-pay clauses set a floor on annual payments regardless of offtake. Both convert system risk into utility risk, and both are reasonable when the utility controls the cause. They become problematic when the utility controls neither the transmission timetable nor demand growth, because then the clause allocates risk to the party that can do least about it.

### Currency

A dollar tariff moves currency risk from the project to a buyer whose revenue is in local currency. The model allows part of the tariff to be denominated in local currency and indexed to local inflation, which shares the risk. Local-currency debt is the other half of the solution. Nachtigal raised a 21-year local-currency tranche guaranteed by IBRD alongside an 18-year euro tranche [@PF-11], and Reventazón paired dollar development-bank and bond debt with local-currency bank loans [@INT:S20]. Neither eliminates the problem, but both reduce the mismatch at its source.

### Termination

Termination provisions are where the largest contingent liabilities hide. A typical formula for termination following government default or political force majeure pays outstanding senior debt plus some compensation to equity. In the model, exposure in each year equals debt outstanding plus unrecovered private equity times one plus a premium, 15 percent by default. For Lumora under the PPP structure, this is roughly USD {{cmp.term.3|n0}} million at commercial operation in the closed-form comparison. Few ministries record this number anywhere.

# Part V | The finance layer

## Chapter 11. Sizing hydro debt

### Tenor

For an asset that can run for fifty to a hundred years, the length of the debt matters more for the tariff than almost any other financing term. Commercial tenors for African hydro are rarely longer than fifteen to eighteen years, as Ebobisse and Hott noted [@PF-13]. Nachtigal's tranches ran eighteen and twenty-one years with a grace period of about six years [@PF-11].

Bujagali shows the cost of getting it wrong. Its original debt tenor was short, so capacity charges were front-loaded and the tariff was high in the early years. The 2018 refinancing extended maturity and lowered the tariff, but the state paid part of the price: the 30 percent corporation tax was waived in exchange for the re-profiling, and the return on equity was cut from 19 to a proposed 15 percent [@A2:S50; @A2:S51].

### Sculpting without circularity

The model sizes commercial debt by sculpting debt service to a target coverage ratio on a lender case. In each loan year *k*,

$$ DS^m^~k~ = max( 0, CFADS^L^~k~ / DSCR* - DS^c^~k~ ) $$ (11.1)

where *CFADS^L^* is lender-case cash flow (P90 generation), *DSCR\** the target ratio and *DS^c^* concessional debt service, which is fixed by the concessional loan's own terms. Debt capacity is the present value of that stream at the commercial interest rate *r*:

$$ D^m^~max~ = Σ~k~ DS^m^~k~ / (1 + r)^k^ $$ (11.2)

and commercial debt is the lesser of capacity and the gearing limit less concessional debt.

A model that sizes debt from cash flows that depend on interest, which depends on debt, is circular. Practitioners often break the loop with a macro or with Excel's iterative calculation. We chose to avoid it by construction. Interest during construction, upfront fees and the initial debt service reserve are funded by equity, and the lender case computes tax on depreciation that excludes those items. Both choices are slightly conservative. In practice IDC is often debt-funded, which would raise debt and lower equity somewhat.

For stress testing, the user can lock the debt amount and switch to an annuity profile. That is what lenders do: once a package is sized, they ask how it performs when things go wrong, not how much they would lend in a bad world.

### Covenants

We found little institutional evidence on covenant levels for African hydro. The most specific public source is Nachtigal's appraisal document, which reports a lenders' base-case minimum DSCR of 1.45 and an average of 1.53, described as consistent with precedent [@PF-11]. The model's sizing ratios, between 1.20 and 1.35 depending on structure, and its distribution lock-up at 1.10 are therefore user inputs, not sourced norms.

## Chapter 12. Five structures, one project

### The structures

The model holds five financing structures for the same plant.

1. A public structure, in which a state-owned company builds and owns the plant with debt borrowed by the sovereign.
2. An IPP, in which a private sponsor builds, owns and operates the plant under a thirty-year concession.
3. A PPP in which a state-owned company and a private sponsor share the equity and development finance institutions provide concessional debt.
4. A hybrid, in which the state funds the civil works and a private company finances the electro-mechanical equipment and operates the plant.
5. A blended structure, with grants, concessional debt and private capital in proportions typical of climate-finance packages.

Each structure has its own debt terms, equity mix, guarantee package and target return.

### A closed-form comparison

Before running the full engine, it is useful to ask what tariff each structure would need to recover its costs. Treating the funding as a level annuity over the thirty-year concession, the required tariff for structure *s* is

$$ τ~s~ = [ K (1 - γ~s~) × CRF(w~s~, N) + O ] / E~P50~ $$ (12.1)

where *K* is the funding base, γ~s~ the grant share, *w~s~* the cost of capital on non-grant funding, *CRF* the capital recovery factor over *N* years, and *O* annual operating cost. Government equity is costed at the government's discount rate, not at zero, because public money has an opportunity cost.

### What the comparison shows

The required tariff depends mostly on the cost of capital. The IPP needs about USD {{cmp.tariff.2|n0}} per MWh, the public structure about USD {{cmp.tariff.1|n0}}. The difference is the price of private risk-taking, and whether it is worth paying depends on whether the private sponsor actually takes risks the state would otherwise carry, such as construction and operating performance.

Gross public exposure at commercial operation barely moves, between about USD {{x.pubexp_min|n0}} million and USD {{x.pubexp_max|n0}} million, or roughly {{cmp.pubexp_pct.3|pct1}} of GDP, across all five structures. The public structure carries it as direct debt. The IPP carries it as a termination obligation and a payment guarantee. The PPP and the blended structure carry it as a mix of public capital, guaranteed debt and termination exposure. The choice of structure changes the form and timing of the state's exposure far more than its size.

### The full engine

The closed form holds the tariff at cost recovery. The full engine holds it at the negotiated PPA level, which is the same for every structure in our runs. That difference produces two results that matter for policy.

First, grants without a tariff adjustment are a windfall. In the hybrid structure, the state contributes 35 percent of the funding base as a capital grant. At the unchanged PPA tariff, the private equity return rises to {{s4.kpi_eirr|pct1}} while the fiscal NPV falls to about USD {{s4.fis_npv|n0}} million. A grant or viability gap payment should be matched by a lower tariff or an explicit cap on returns. Otherwise public money flows to private shareholders.

Second, public ownership at an IPP-level tariff looks attractive to the treasury and expensive to consumers. In the public structure, fiscal NPV is about USD {{s1.fis_npv|n0}} million at the same tariff, because the state receives all equity returns. Consumers, meanwhile, pay a blended tariff of about USD {{x.blend_y2|n0}} per MWh in the second operating year, well above the public structure's cost-recovery level of about USD {{cmp.tariff.1|n0}}.
