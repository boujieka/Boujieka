**Bankable Hydro.** Structuring hydropower projects without creating unsustainable public liabilities.

Africa Energy Finance Series, Volume 7. First edition, October 2026.

This book is accompanied by the Bankable Hydro Integrated Bankability Model (Excel workbook, 35 sheets), a user manual, a course workbook and a case library. All references to "the model" are to that workbook.

The reference project used throughout, the Lumora Falls Hydropower Project in the Republic of Navaria, is fictional. Its river, utility, government, prices and contracts were constructed for teaching. Any resemblance to a real project is coincidental. Real projects appear only as benchmarks, drawn from public documents that are listed in the references; where a public figure could not be confirmed, the text says so.

Nothing in this book is investment, legal, tax or accounting advice. Readers who apply the methods to a real transaction should rely on their own advisers and on the project's own documents.

## Preface

Most of the hydropower projects that stall in Africa do not stall because the engineering is wrong. They stall because somebody further down the chain cannot carry the risk that the engineering leaves behind. The river is real, the dam can be built, the turbines will turn. Then the questions start. Who will buy the power, and with what money? Will the line to the load centre be finished on time, and who pays if it is not? What happens to a dollar tariff when the local currency loses a third of its value in a year? And when the lenders insist on guarantees, who in the Ministry of Finance has added them up?

This book is about those questions. It treats a hydropower project as one link in a chain that runs from the river to the national budget, and it asks whether the whole chain holds. The test we propose is simple to state and hard to pass: a project is ready for financial close only when it can deliver power that lenders will finance *and* when the commitments the state makes to get it financed are affordable under plausible stress.

We wrote it for people who sit on different sides of the same table. Developers and sponsors who want to know what lenders will ask before they ask it. Lenders and development finance institutions who need to see where a payment shortfall would actually come from. Ministry of Finance and PPP unit staff who are asked to sign guarantees and implementation agreements, often with little time and less analysis. Utility managers who will pay the bill for thirty years. Transaction advisers who have to reconcile all of them.

Three choices shaped the book. First, every claim about a real project is tied to a public source, and where we could not find one we say "public data not found" rather than estimate. Second, every number about the reference project comes from the companion model, so the text and the spreadsheet cannot drift apart. Third, we reviewed our own work adversarially before publishing it, and Chapter 18 reports what that review found, including the weaknesses we fixed and the ones we could not.

A word on tone. Hydropower finance attracts strong views. Large dams have their defenders and their critics, and both camps can point to evidence. This book takes no side on whether a given country should build a given dam. It takes a narrower position: if a country decides to build one, the deal should be structured so that its costs and risks are visible to the people who will eventually bear them.
