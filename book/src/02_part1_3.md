# Part I | The problem and the method

## Chapter 1. Feasible, bankable, sustainable

### Three ways a hydro project fails

A hydropower project can fail at three different points, and the three are easy to confuse.

The first is the familiar one. The scheme is technically feasible, the feasibility study is solid, and still no lender will commit. Usually the reason sits outside the plant. The buyer of the power has a record of paying late, the evacuation line has no financing, or the contract framework cannot be enforced in a way that a commercial bank's credit committee would accept.

The second failure is quieter. Lenders do commit, but only after the risk they would not carry has been moved somewhere else, almost always onto the state. A payment guarantee covers the utility. A loan guarantee covers the lenders. A termination clause promises that if the government defaults or a political event ends the concession, the state will repay the debt and compensate the equity. Each of these instruments makes the project bankable. None of them shows up as public debt on the day it is signed.

The third failure comes years later, when the commitments start to bite. The utility falls behind on its payments to the IPP. A guarantee is called. Energy that cannot be evacuated is paid for anyway under a deemed-energy clause. Arrears on a sovereign-guaranteed construction loan pile up. What was a power-sector problem becomes a fiscal one.

The public record contains all three. Zambia's Kafue Gorge Lower plant saw its cost rise from about USD 1.48 billion to about USD 2 billion [@A1:S57], and the final figure includes roughly USD 312 million of capitalised interest [@A1:S58]. By the time of the 2024 joint IMF and World Bank debt sustainability analysis, payables owed by the national utility ZESCO to independent producers, about USD 1.2 billion of domestic arrears, had been brought inside the sovereign debt perimeter [@DB-03]. Outside Africa, the World Bank reports that Lao PDR's public and publicly guaranteed debt reached 112 percent of GDP at the end of 2022, with the energy sector accounting for about 43 percent of it [@INT:S13]. Much of that debt was built through take-or-pay contracts signed by a financially weak domestic utility.

These are not isolated cases. Across Sub-Saharan Africa, Trimble and co-authors estimated that the quasi-fiscal deficit of the power sector, the gap between what an efficient utility would need to recover its costs and what it actually collects, averaged about 1.5 percent of GDP and exceeded 5 percent in several countries [@UT-02]. A World Bank review of fiscal risk in the power sector found that annual fiscal injections into power SOEs averaged 0.25 percent of GDP and that between 24 and 41 percent of power PPP contracts were renegotiated [@FR-17].

### The argument of this book

We argue that a hydropower project should be judged against three tests at once. The first is feasibility: can the plant be built and run to produce the energy claimed, at the cost claimed? The second is bankability: will commercial and development lenders finance it on terms the project can service, given the risks they are asked to carry? The third is fiscal sustainability, and it is the one most often skipped. Are the commitments the state makes to secure that financing affordable, including under the stresses that history tells us to expect?

Most analytical tools address one test at a time. Engineering studies answer the first. Project finance models answer the second, usually on the assumption that the offtaker pays. Fiscal risk tools such as the IMF and World Bank PFRAM answer the third at portfolio level, without a hydrological or utility layer [@FR-01]. Each tool is good at what it does. The difficulty is that the risks pass between them. A transmission delay that the project model ignores because the PPA pays deemed energy reappears as a utility cash shortfall, which reappears as a budget transfer or a guarantee call. If the three analyses are done by different teams on different assumptions, nobody sees the whole path.

### What the book adds

We make four contributions.

The first is a single analytical engine, implemented in the companion model, that follows one set of assumptions from river flow to fiscal exposure. The same inputs drive the project's DSCR, the utility's cash position and the state's contingent liabilities.

The second is a small number of quantities that link the layers and that, as far as our review of existing products found, are not computed together elsewhere [@CI]. The most important are the offtaker's maximum sustainable PPA payment, the payment capacity gap that follows from it, and the transmission readiness gap. Chapters 6 and 8 define them.

The third is an explicit rule for where a shortfall goes. When the utility cannot pay, the money comes from a budget transfer, from a guarantee, or from nobody, in which case it becomes arrears owed to the project. The model routes every shortfall through that sequence, so a reader can see who ends up holding it in each scenario.

The fourth is the Hydropower Bankability Framework, a set of nine gates with published thresholds and a weakest-link rule. It is designed to be argued with. Every threshold is visible and editable, and Chapter 16 explains why we chose each one.

### How to read the numbers

Every figure about the fictional Lumora Falls project comes from the companion model, run in the configuration stated in the text. The base case uses the PPP structure, a P50 generation case for cash flows and a P90 case for debt sizing. Stress cases are run with the financing package locked at its base-case terms, because that is how lenders and sponsors test a package they have already signed. Where a figure says "about", it has been rounded from a model output, not estimated.

## Chapter 2. A chain of risks

### From river to budget

The chain this book follows has fifteen links:

hydrological resource, power plant, transmission, grid, demand, offtaker, utility, regulation, PPA, financing, PPP or IPP structure, government support, fiscal exposure, debt sustainability, financial close.

It is tempting to treat these as a checklist. They behave more like a pipe. Risk enters at one end, mostly as physical and commercial uncertainty about water, construction and demand, and it travels along the pipe until somebody absorbs it. Contracts determine where it stops. Very little of it disappears.

### Risk is moved, not removed

Consider what a deemed-energy clause does. Suppose the transmission line is two years late. Without the clause, the project loses revenue and its lenders lose cover. With the clause, the utility pays for energy it cannot receive, so the project is whole and the lenders are content. The risk has not gone. It sits on the utility's income statement. If the utility cannot afford it, the government either tops up the utility or honours a payment guarantee. If the government does neither, the utility defaults on the PPA and the risk returns to the project, now in the form of arrears, which is worse than lost revenue because it comes with a dispute attached.

This is what we mean by saying risk is conserved. Contracts allocate it. Sometimes they allocate it to the party best able to manage it, which is the textbook ideal. Sometimes they allocate it to the party least able to refuse, which is closer to what happens when a single-buyer utility and a finance ministry face a well-advised sponsor with a deadline.

### Form and substance

Off-balance-sheet structures make the point sharper. The Reventazón plant in Costa Rica is owned by a trust and leased to ICE, the state utility. In form, the asset and its debt sit outside ICE. In substance, construction, operating and overrun risk pass through to ICE under the lease, which is why rating analysts described the transaction as lightly structured ICE risk [@INT:S20]. The structure did useful things. It allowed project-style financing, including an investment-grade bond and local-currency bank debt. It did not change who carried the risk.

We return to this in Chapter 12, where the model runs five financing structures for the same Lumora plant. Gross public exposure at commercial operation varies surprisingly little across them. What varies is the form the exposure takes and when it can be called.

### Notation

We use a small amount of notation. Time is indexed in years, *t*, from financial close. Operating years are indexed separately, *k* = 1, 2, … from commercial operation. Money is in millions of nominal US dollars unless stated. *E~t~* is energy in GWh, *R~t~* revenue, *O~t~* operating cost, *T~t~* tax and *DS~t~* senior debt service. The DSCR in year *t* is

$$ DSCR~t~ = CFADS~t~ / DS~t~, where CFADS~t~ = R~t~ - O~t~ - T~t~ $$ (2.1)

Other symbols are defined where they are used.

# Part II | The project layer

## Chapter 3. Hydrology

### Four kinds of capacity

Financing documents are full of capacity numbers, and they do not mean the same thing. It helps to keep four apart.

Installed capacity is the nameplate rating of the generating units. Available capacity is installed capacity multiplied by the share of time the units can run, after planned and forced outages. Expected generation is the energy the river allows on average, which depends on flow, head and how often the flow exceeds what the turbines can use. Contracted energy is whatever the PPA refers to, and it may differ both from what is delivered, because of curtailment, and from what is paid for, because of deemed-energy and take-or-pay clauses.

The gap between nameplate and energy can be very large. Belo Monte in Brazil has 11,233 MW installed against 4,571 MW of assured energy, about 41 percent of nameplate [@INT:S8]. That is an extreme case, the result of a run-of-river design on a river with a pronounced dry season, but it shows why a lender looks past the MW figure.

### From flow to energy

For each month, the model computes usable flow, average power and energy:

$$ Q~u~ = min( max(Q - Q~e~, 0), Q~d~ ) $$ (3.1)

$$ P = min( ρ g Q~u~ H η~t~ η~g~ / 10^6^ , P~inst~ ) $$ (3.2)

$$ E = P × hours × availability / 1000 $$ (3.3)

Here *Q* is mean river flow, *Q~e~* the environmental flow release, *Q~d~* the rated turbine flow, *H* the net head, η~t~ and η~g~ the turbine and generator efficiencies, and *P~inst~* the installed capacity. Power is in MW and energy in GWh.

For Lumora, mean monthly flows range from 120 to 520 m³/s, the design flow is 450 m³/s and the net head is 105 m. The result is a long-term mean, or P50, of about {{m.p50|n0}} GWh a year and a capacity factor of {{m.cf|pct1}}. That sits close to the weighted average of 48 percent that IRENA reports for large hydro commissioned in Africa between 2018 and 2024, inside a 5th to 95th percentile band of 33 to 81 percent [@HY-01].

!fig|book/figures/fig3_1_hydrology.png|Figure 3.1. Lumora Falls: mean monthly flow and expected monthly energy. Flow above the 450 m³/s design flow is spilled; flow below the environmental release generates nothing. Source: companion model, sheet 03.

There is a bias hidden in equations 3.1 to 3.3 that matters for finance. Energy is a concave function of flow: it rises with flow until the turbines are full and then stops rising. Applying the function to *mean* monthly flows therefore overstates mean energy, because wet years cannot make up for dry years once the turbines are saturated. This is Jensen's inequality, and its size depends on how often flows cross the design flow. For a bankable estimate, the right procedure is to compute energy for each month of the historical record and average the results. The model flags this and accepts a simulated series if one is available.

### P50, P75 and P90

Lenders size debt on a downside generation case. Practice varies, but the common reference is a P90 estimate, the annual energy exceeded in nine years out of ten. Without a full simulated series, the model uses a normal approximation driven by the coefficient of variation of annual energy:

$$ P90 = P50 × (1 - 1.2816 × CV),   P75 = P50 × (1 - 0.6745 × CV) $$ (3.4)

With a CV of 12 percent, Lumora's P90 is about {{m.p90|n0}} GWh, a P90 to P50 ratio of {{m.p90_p50|n2}}.

Two cautions apply. The first is distributional. Annual hydro output is often skewed, with a longer tail on the dry side than the normal distribution allows, so the approximation can understate downside risk. The second is about horizon. A one-year P90 describes a single bad year. Debt service coverage over a tenor of fifteen or twenty years depends more on the average of many years, whose distribution is tighter, and on the risk of several dry years in a row, whose distribution is not captured by any single-year statistic. Applying a one-year P90 to every year of the loan life is conservative for the average and silent about persistence. We return to this in Chapter 18.

### Drought and climate

Kariba is the reminder that persistence matters. In 2024 the Zambezi River Authority cut the water allocation for generation at Kariba from 30 to 16 billion cubic metres, according to news reporting at the time [@CL-04]. A drought of that kind does not produce one bad year followed by recovery. It produces a sequence.

Peer-reviewed work adds a regional dimension. Conway and co-authors found that planned hydropower expansion in eastern and southern Africa clusters capacity in basins with correlated rainfall, which raises the risk of concurrent climate-related supply disruption across interconnected systems [@CL-03]. A national power system that relies on several hydro plants in the same climate regime can lose a large share of its supply at once.

The model offers two hydrological stresses. A drought window applies a flow factor for a chosen number of consecutive operating years. The default, 55 percent of normal flow for three years, is set close to the cut in Kariba's 2024 allocation. A climate trend applies a percentage change in mean flow per decade, which we label illustrative until a basin-specific climate study supports a number. The IHA climate resilience guide sets out how such studies should be done [@CL-01].

With the financing package fixed at its base-case terms, a three-year drought at 55 percent of normal flow reduces Lumora's minimum DSCR from {{base_locked.kpi_min_dscr|x2}} to {{drought.kpi_min_dscr|x2}}. The equity return moves by less than a point, from {{base_locked.kpi_eirr|pct1}} to {{drought.kpi_eirr|pct1}}. The reason is the tariff. About {{m.rev_fixed|pct0}} of Lumora's revenue comes from capacity payments, which do not depend on water. The project is protected. Chapter 10 asks who pays for that protection.

## Chapter 4. Cost, construction and the reference class

### Benchmarks and their limits

IRENA's cost database gives a weighted-average installed cost for large hydro in Africa of USD 2,330 per kW for projects commissioned between 2010 and 2017 and USD 2,515 per kW for 2018 to 2024, in 2024 dollars [@HY-01]. The World Bank appraisal of Nachtigal in Cameroon implies about USD 3,290 per kW, from a total of USD 1,383 million for 420 MW [@PF-11].

Unit costs in our case library spread much wider, from roughly USD 1,000 per kW for the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam to more than USD 5,800 per kW for the 80 MW Rusumo Falls scheme. Those figures should not be compared directly. They are in the nominal currency of different years and they cover different scopes. Some include interest during construction, transmission and resettlement, and some do not.

Scope is the larger problem. The approved budget for Three Gorges ranged from 57 billion to 205 billion yuan depending on whether resettlement, grid connection and financing costs were counted [@INT:S2]. A cost estimate that does not say what it includes cannot be checked. The model reports four cost measures separately: plant capital cost, the funding base (capital cost plus any project-built transmission), total uses (adding IDC, fees and the initial debt reserve), and system cost, which adds publicly funded transmission. For Lumora these are about {{m.capex_real|n0}} million real 2026 dollars of plant cost, {{m.u_capex|n0}} million nominal, and {{m.uses|n0}} million of total uses.

### The reference class

The single most useful number in hydropower cost estimation comes from outside any one project. Ansar, Flyvbjerg, Budzier and Lunn studied 245 large dams built in 65 countries and found a median real cost overrun of 27 percent and a mean of 96 percent. Schedules overran by a median of 27 percent, or 1.7 years, and by 44 percent, or 2.3 years, on average. The average implementation time was 8.6 years [@HY-08].

The distribution is heavily skewed. The median tells you what to expect; the mean tells you how bad the tail is. African experience fits the pattern.

| Project | Reported overrun or delay | Source |
|---|---|---|

The model's default overrun stress is the median, 27 percent. That choice deserves comment. It means the stress case is roughly what an outside view would treat as an expected outcome, not a tail event. A user who thinks the project sits in the right-hand part of the distribution, because the geology is poorly investigated or the procurement is rushed, should run 50 percent or more.

Applied to Lumora with the financing package fixed, the 27 percent overrun opens a financing gap of USD {{overrun.fin_gap|n0}} million, where the base case has none, and cuts the equity return to {{overrun.kpi_eirr|pct1}}. On the public side, the central-government fiscal NPV falls from about USD {{base_locked.fis_npv|n0}} million to USD {{overrun.fis_npv|n0}} million, mainly because the public transmission line costs more, and peak contingent exposure rises to about USD {{overrun.cl_peak|n0}} million. At the reference-class mean of 96 percent, the picture is of a different kind: the gap reaches USD {{overrun96.fin_gap|n0}} million, the equity return falls to {{overrun96.kpi_eirr|pct1}} and contingent exposure peaks near USD {{overrun96.cl_peak|n0}} million. No sponsor would fund that gap without new public money, which is how overruns on this scale usually end.

### Delay

Delay costs money twice. During construction, interest and escalation accumulate. After construction, revenue arrives later, so every dollar is worth less. At Kafue Gorge Lower, capitalised interest of USD 312 million made up about 16 percent of a USD 2 billion total [@A1:S58]. The model adds a delay cost of 4 percent of capital cost per year of delay, extends the construction profile, and recomputes IDC. A two-year delay opens a financing gap of about USD {{delay.fin_gap|n0}} million and lowers the equity return to {{delay.kpi_eirr|pct1}}.

## Chapter 5. Operating cost, tariff and the cost of electricity

### Operating costs

Hydropower is cheap to run. IRENA's sample suggests fixed operation and maintenance costs of 1 to 3 percent of installed cost a year, with an average a little under 2 percent and lower ratios for large plants [@HY-01]. Lumora's fixed costs, including insurance and a major maintenance reserve, come to roughly 2 percent. Two items that are easy to forget are the water-use royalty, which is a revenue stream for the state, and the community and environmental monitoring programmes that lenders' E&S standards require.

### Plant cost and system cost

The model computes two levelised costs of electricity. The plant LCOE divides the present value of plant capital and operating costs by the present value of energy that is delivered or paid for as deemed energy. The system LCOE adds publicly funded transmission capital and operating costs and divides by energy delivered to load, net of transmission losses.

$$ LCOE~plant~ = PV(C~plant~) / PV(E~paid~),   LCOE~system~ = PV(C~plant~ + C~tx,public~) / PV(E~load~) $$ (5.1)

At a 10 percent nominal discount rate, Lumora's plant LCOE is about USD {{m.lcoe|n0}} per MWh and its system LCOE about USD {{m.lcoe_sys|n0}} per MWh. The difference is a real cost that sits on the public side of the ledger and that a plant-only analysis never sees. In negotiations between a sponsor and a ministry, it is common for the sponsor to quote the first number and for the ministry to pay the second.

A note on the discount rate. A nominal levelised cost at 10 percent is a convention, not a truth. IRENA's own cost of capital assumptions range from 3.8 percent in Europe to 12 percent in Africa, in real terms [@HY-01]. Readers comparing Lumora's LCOE with published figures should check the discount rate and whether the figure is real or nominal before drawing conclusions.

# Part III | The power-system layer

## Chapter 6. Transmission and the readiness gap

### Can the plant evacuate what it generates?

The question sounds too obvious to ask. It is asked too rarely. The plant and its evacuation line are often procured by different entities, financed by different lenders and built to different timetables, and the interface between them is where a large share of value is lost.

Rusumo Falls illustrates the coordination problem. Generation was financed by IDA grants and credits to the three riparian states; transmission was financed in parallel by the African Development Bank and the European Union [@A2:S16]. Supply into the grids depended on cross-border lines and three separate power purchase agreements. Uganda illustrates the financial consequence. In 2020, according to press reporting that we could see only in search results, about 9 percent of the transmission company UETCL's purchases were deemed energy, paid for but not evacuated [@A2:S55].

### Measuring readiness

We define the transmission readiness gap in two parts. The timing gap is the transmission line's commercial operation date minus the plant's. The capacity gap is installed capacity minus the evacuation capacity available in the plant's first operating year.

Evacuation capacity in each year is the smaller of what the line can carry and what the grid can absorb:

$$ EVAC~t~ = min( LINE~t~ , ABSORB~t~ ) $$ (6.1)

where *LINE~t~* is the line capacity once the line is in service and the interim capacity of the existing network before that.

Grid absorption at minimum load is approximated as minimum load minus must-run generation plus export interconnector capacity. Delivered energy is then

$$ E^del^~t~ = min( E~t~ × min(1, EVAC~t~ / P~inst~) , ABSORBABLE DEMAND~t~ ) $$ (6.2)

The proportional rule in equation 6.2 assumes curtailment scales with the shortfall in evacuation capacity. For a plant with daily pondage that can shift output into hours when the line has room, this is conservative. A dispatch study would do better.

### Cost of the line

The Eastern Africa Power Pool master plan lists 400 kV AC line costs between about USD 0.36 and 0.70 million per km, in 2012 dollars and including IDC [@TX-04]. Lumora's 180 km line at USD 0.55 million per km, plus substations and downstream reinforcement, costs about USD {{m.tx_capex_real|n0}} million in real 2026 terms, roughly a fifth of plant cost and about USD {{m.tx_cost_kw|n0}} per kW of plant.

### Who pays when the line is late

That depends on two contract choices: who builds the line, and whether the PPA pays deemed energy.

In the Lumora base case the state builds the line and the PPA pays deemed energy. With a two-year transmission delay and the financing package fixed, the IPP is untouched: its minimum DSCR stays at {{trans.kpi_min_dscr|x2}}, because the utility pays for energy it cannot receive. The utility's payment capacity ratio falls from {{base_locked.ut_ratio10|x2}} to {{trans.ut_ratio10|x2}}, and Gate 4 drops to critical.

Where the cost lands depends on how wide one draws the public sector. Central-government fiscal NPV hardly moves, from USD {{base_locked.fis_npv|n0}} million to USD {{trans.fis_npv|n0}} million. The consolidated figure, which includes the state utility, falls from USD {{base_locked.fis_npv_cons|n0}} million to USD {{trans.fis_npv_cons|n0}} million, because the utility pays deemed energy while still buying replacement power. An earlier version of our model, which stopped at the Treasury, showed the delay as fiscally beneficial. Chapter 18 explains the correction. The general point stands: a fiscal indicator that excludes the state utility will miss exactly the costs that power-sector contracts are designed to push onto it.

## Chapter 7. Grid and demand

### Four kinds of demand

A load forecast can mean four different things, and only one of them supports a loan.

Table: Table 7.1. Demand concepts used in the model
| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| Potential | Sum of segment demand (residential, commercial, industrial, mining, productive use, public) plus export potential |
| Commercial | Segment demand weighted by the share coming from customers able and willing to pay cost-reflective tariffs |
| Contracted | Energy covered by the PPA, at P50 |
| Bankable | The lesser of contracted energy and absorbable energy scaled by the commercial share |

Absorbable energy is the supply gap left after other committed plants, plus existing thermal generation the project can displace, plus signed export contracts. The distinction between potential and commercial demand is where electrification ambitions meet balance sheets. A household that would use electricity if it were connected is potential demand. A household that is connected and pays its bill is commercial demand.

### Building ahead of demand

Tanzania shows the risk of building ahead of demand. With Julius Nyerere's 2,115 MW in service, national installed capacity reached about 4.6 GW against a peak demand of about 2.27 GW, a surplus of 2.38 GW, and exports were being discussed to absorb it [@A2:S30]. A surplus of that size is not a failure if demand grows into it. It is a fiscal question in the meantime, because somebody pays for capacity that is not used.

### Grid integration

A plant that supplies a large share of system peak raises questions about reserves, frequency control and what happens when it trips. Lumora at commercial operation is about {{m.share_cod|pct0}} of national peak. The model treats a share above 15 percent as conditional and above 25 percent as a development gap. These are screening thresholds, not engineering limits, and a grid-impact study should replace them.
